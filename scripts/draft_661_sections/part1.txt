## 1. The Disciplinary Dilemma: Generative AI, Aural Skills, and Harmonic Analysis in High School AP Music Theory

High School AP Music Theory occupies a uniquely demanding position in secondary education. Unlike standard humanities courses that rely strictly on textual synthesis, or STEM courses centered on mathematical derivations, AP Music Theory requires students to seamlessly unite two fundamentally different cognitive domains:

1. **Aural & Psychoacoustic Skills:** Real-time auditory processing, pitch interval recognition, rhythmic subdivision, melodic dictation over multi-pass listening cycles, sight-singing, and harmonic dictation tracking the outer soprano-bass counterpoint.
2. **Structural & Compositional Written Rules:** Rigorous 18th-century common-practice voice leading, part-writing from figured bass and Roman numerals, realization of four-part SATB (Soprano, Alto, Tenor, Bass) textures, identification of phrase cadences (PAC, IAC, HC, DC, Plagal), and advanced chromatic analysis (secondary dominants, Neapolitan chords, augmented sixth chords).

In a typical high school setting—where music directors and AP theory instructors balance 120–160 students across concert bands, symphony orchestras, choirs, and advanced academic theory sections—the grading workload is formidable. Teachers regularly assign take-home score analyses, digital sight-singing reflections, harmonic error-detection writeups, and melodic dictation self-assessments via 1:1 Chromebooks, Google Docs, Canvas SpeedGrader, Schoology, and Buzz LMS.

### The Arrival of Multimodal AI in the High School Music Classroom

With the rapid emergence of multimodal LLMs capable of analyzing uploaded score images and generating natural-language text, high school theory students frequently outsource their analytical homework. When confronted with a challenging Bach chorale excerpt, a complex Mozart keyboard exposition, or an unfamiliar melodic dictation audio file, a student can upload a screenshot or type a brief prompt into an AI assistant.

Within seconds, the chatbot generates a polished analytical narrative. The generated writeup fluently discusses tonic prolongations, subdominant preparations, deceptive resolutions, and passing tones. 

However, because generative LLMs operate on statistical token probability rather than auditory audition or rule-based counterpoint validation, they routinely make catastrophic music-theoretical errors:
- **Confusing Relative Keys:** Treating D minor as F major or A minor as C major because they share identical key signatures.
- **Inventing Phantom Chord Inversions:** Labeling a standard root-position dominant seventh (`V7`) as a third-inversion four-two chord (`V4/2`) without bass note verification.
- **Ignoring Part-Writing Voice-Leading Rules:** Praising voice leading that contains obvious parallel fifths, parallel octaves, voice crossing, or unresolved leading tones.
- **Failing Melodic Dictation Realism:** Producing complete transcriptions in a single instant with zero evidence of the cyclical, multi-pass listening strategy mandatory in AP Music Theory.
