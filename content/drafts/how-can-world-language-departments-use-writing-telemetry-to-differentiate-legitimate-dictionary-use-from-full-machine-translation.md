### 1. Swain’s Output Hypothesis and the ACTFL Proficiency Continuum

In SLA research, Merrill Swain’s seminal **Output Hypothesis** demonstrates that language acquisition does not occur through passive input alone; it requires **active communicative output**. When learners produce written output in a target language ($L_2$), they encounter three vital cognitive mechanisms:
* **The Noticing/Triggering Function:** Learners notice the gap between what they wish to convey and what their current interlanguage grammar permits them to produce.
* **The Hypothesis-Testing Function:** Learners test grammatical rules (e.g., "Does this verb require the subjunctive in French after *bien que*?") and refine their internal mental models based on feedback.
* **The Metalinguistic Function:** Learners reflect on syntactic relationships, forcing their brains to transition from semantic processing (understanding meaning) to syntactic processing (constructing form).

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### 1. Does tracking keystroke telemetry violate student privacy laws like FERPA or COPPA?
No. Checkmark Plagiarism is fully **FERPA and COPPA compliant**. Telemetric data (keystroke timing, paste buffer contents, revision timestamps) is captured strictly within the educational institution’s secure digital perimeter. Checkmark enforces a strict **Zero-Model-Training Policy**: student writing and telemetry data are **never** used to train public or commercial AI models, sold to third parties, or shared across institutions.

### 2. How does keystroke telemetry handle logographic and character-based languages like Mandarin Chinese or Japanese?
In character-based languages, students use **Input Method Editors (IMEs)** (e.g., Pinyin for Mandarin, Romaji/Kana for Japanese) to type phonetic strings that convert into Hanzi or Kanji characters. Checkmark’s telemetry engine is specifically calibrated for IMEs: it captures the conversion selection pause (the micro-hesitation while selecting the correct character from a candidate list) as authentic drafting friction, easily distinguishing manual IME character selection from multi-sentence machine-translated paste drops.

### 3. How does writing telemetry differentiate between English Language Learners (ELL) and World Language ($L_2$) students?
While the underlying telemetry technology is identical, the instructional objectives differ. In ELL contexts, students frequently use translation tools to bridge vocabulary into English for general academic content. In World Language classrooms, the **target language output itself is the primary object of assessment**. Checkmark allows teachers to customize threshold sensitivity, ensuring language educators can differentiate isolated dictionary lookups from unauthorized full-clause translation.

### 4. Can students bypass telemetry by using browser extensions or automated typing scripts?
Automated typing scripts ("paste-as-type" extensions) simulate keystrokes, but they produce a perfectly uniform, isochronous Inter-Key Interval (e.g., exactly 50ms between every character). Human typing in a foreign language is inherently variable, marked by natural cognitive pauses before difficult vocabulary, irregular verb endings, and accents. Checkmark's cadence anomaly detectors instantly flag robotic, non-human typing patterns.

### 5. What happens if a student writes their draft on paper first and then types it into the computer?
If a student drafts on paper and types their handwritten notes into the digital editor, Essay Playback™ will reflect continuous typing with occasional pauses as they look down at their notebook. However, to eliminate ambiguity, world language departments should establish a clear policy: students are encouraged to compose directly within the digital writing environment, or retain their physical handwritten notes to present during an oral check-in if requested.

### 6. How does Checkmark integrate with Canvas SpeedGrader, Buzz LMS, and Google Classroom?
Checkmark embeds directly into the teacher's grading workflow. Within Canvas SpeedGrader, Buzz LMS, or Google Classroom, educators can launch the Essay Playback™ timeline, review the Paste Buffer Inspector, and view passage-level AI detection flags directly alongside the submission. Rubric autograder scores and feedback can be pushed to the LMS gradebook with a single click.

### 7. Why is a single whole-document percentage score dangerous for foreign language essays?
Generic AI detectors calculate a single probability score based on English-centric statistical metrics (perplexity and burstiness). Because beginning and intermediate language learners naturally write with simple sentence patterns and limited vocabulary, their authentic writing closely mimics the mathematical profile of generic AI text. Relying on a single percentage leads to devastating false-positive accusations against honest students. Checkmark eliminates this danger by replacing opaque scores with transparent, visual, and multi-dimensional drafting evidence.
