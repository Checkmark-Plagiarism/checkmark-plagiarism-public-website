### The Failure of Blanket "Zero-AI" Bans

When generative AI entered mainstream educational discourse, many institutional leaders and departmental committees reacted by issuing categorical, blanket bans: *"The use of Artificial Intelligence or automated writing tools of any kind is strictly prohibited and constitutes an honor code violation."*

1. **Ubiquitous Operating System and Platform Integration:** Generative and assistive AI features are now native components of modern operating systems (macOS Tahoe, Windows 11), web browsers (Google Chrome, Microsoft Edge), and word processors (Google Docs, Microsoft Word). Requiring a student to guarantee that "zero AI" interacted with their document requires them to disable core operating system utilities, grammar checkers, and accessibility accommodations.
2. **The Disproportionate Penalization of International and ESL/ELL Students:** Non-native English speakers, multilingual writers, and first-generation college students frequently rely on grammar assistants to navigate unfamiliar academic idiom, verify prepositional usage, and refine syntactic cadence. Categorical bans strip these students of legitimate scaffolding, forcing them into constant anxiety regarding disciplinary repercussions.
3. **The Subjectivity of Faculty Enforcement:** In a writing program comprising 50 to 200 full-time professors, contingent lecturers, graduate teaching assistants (GTAs), and adjunct instructors, a vague policy leads to radical grading variance. While one instructor permits Grammarly for final proofreading, an adjacent instructor in the same course flags any student who uses sentence rephrasing as an academic integrity violator, subjecting students to capricious grading standards across sections.

### The Inadequacy of Commercial "AI vs. Human" Percentage Scores

The crisis is exacerbated by commercial plagiarism and AI detection platforms that market opaque, monolithic percentage scores (e.g., *"This paper is 78% AI"*). 

As computational linguistics research has demonstrated, traditional statistical AI detectors calculate probability based on **perplexity** (lexical predictability) and **burstiness** (sentence length variation). When a student writes an authentic, original essay but accepts 10 or 15 stylistic recommendations from an automated grammar tool—substituting simpler synonyms for complex ones, active voice for passive constructions, or removing filler clauses—the resulting text exhibits uniformly low perplexity and flattened burstiness. 

The monolithic detector flags the entire document as machine-generated. The instructor, lacking granular telemetry, assumes the student prompted ChatGPT to ghostwrite the assignment.

To escape this destructive cycle, Writing Program Administrators must lead their departments away from binary black-box verdicts and toward a **multi-tiered, evidence-based policy framework** underpinned by **writing process telemetry**.

### Deconstructing the Continuum Tiers in the Composition Classroom

To make these policy tiers actionable for faculty and students, WPAs must establish explicit operational boundaries for each level.

#### Tier 1: Surface Orthography & Mechanics
Tier 1 represents the foundational baseline of modern digital literacy. These tools operate strictly as digital proofreaders. They identify misspelled words (e.g., changing *"occured"* to *"occurred"*), flag missed punctuation (e.g., inserting a missing apostrophe in a contraction), and correct capitalization errors. 

From an academic integrity perspective, Tier 1 tools do not alter the student's cognitive voice, syntactic rhythm, or rhetorical intent. Banning Tier 1 tools is counterproductive, disproportionately harms neurodivergent students with dysgraphia or dyslexia, and ignores standard professional writing environments.

#### Tier 2: Sentence-Level Style, Tone & Clarity Suggestions
Tier 2 tools evaluate existing, student-authored sentences to enhance readability and conciseness. A Tier 2 tool might underline a 38-word run-on sentence and suggest: *"Consider splitting this into two sentences,"* or highlight *"due to the fact that"* and suggest replacing it with *"because."*

Crucially, in Tier 2:
* The student has already written the full sentence.
* The assistive algorithm offers localized, discrete token replacements.
* The student retains full rhetorical agency to review the suggestion, accept the replacement, or reject it to preserve their unique voice.

Writing Program Administrators should explicitly protect Tier 2 usage in their foundational policies. Penalizing students for accepting clarity suggestions undermines the pedagogy of revision and creates unnecessary institutional friction.

#### Tier 3: Structural Rewriting, Expansion & Paraphrasing
Tier 3 represents the critical fault line where educational integrity is most frequently compromised. Unlike Tier 2, which suggests discrete word swaps, Tier 3 tools take a poorly formed or fragmented clause and completely regenerate it using advanced sequence-to-sequence neural models. 

When a student highlights a sentence and clicks *"Make this sound academic"* in Grammarly Premium, QuillBot, or Apple Intelligence, the software does not simply fix grammar—it replaces the student's authentic syntactic struggle with synthetic, pre-packaged academic cadence.

WPAs must explicitly designate Tier 3 tools as restricted or requiring mandatory disclosure.

#### Tier 4: Prompt-Driven Ideation, Brainstorming & Outlining
Tier 4 encompasses the deliberate, collaborative use of conversational LLMs (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude) during the invention and planning stages of writing. A student might prompt an LLM: *"I am writing an argumentative essay on renewable energy grid stability in rural municipalities. What are three common economic counterarguments, and what primary sources should I investigate?"*

When utilized transparently under structured instructor guidance, Tier 4 usage can accelerate critical thinking and help students explore diverse perspectives. However, if conducted covertly, Tier 4 ideation frequently leads directly into Tier 5 generative text dumping. 

WPAs should position Tier 4 as a **pedagogical scaffold requiring formal attribution**, instructing students to include an **AI Attribution Appendix** detailing their prompt history, model version, and how they critically evaluated and modified the machine's suggestions.

#### Tier 5: End-to-End Generative Text Synthesis
Tier 5 is unequivocal authorship fraud. It occurs when a student inputs an assignment prompt into an LLM, copies the generated essay or paragraph block, and pastes it into their submission document (or into an LMS editor) as if it were their own original intellectual creation. 

Tier 5 usage violates the core ethical standards of academic scholarship, circumvents all cognitive learning goals, and warrants formal academic integrity adjudication.

### The Physics of Human Writing: Keystroke Dynamics and Cognitive Hesitations

Authentic human writing is an inherently messy, non-linear, and cognitively demanding process. When a human sits down to compose an essay, their keystroke stream exhibits distinct physiological and cognitive markers:

1. **Inter-Key Interval (IKI) Variance:** The time between individual keystrokes is not uniform. When typing familiar words (e.g., *"the"*, *"because"*), a student's IKI might average 120–180 milliseconds. However, when navigating complex transitions or selecting precise terminology, the IKI spikes to 800–2,500 milliseconds.
2. **Cognitive Boundary Pauses:** Human writers pause significantly at clause and sentence boundaries. Cognitive science research demonstrates that human writers pause for 2 to 15 seconds at the end of sentences to formulate the next proposition.
3. **Macro-Revision and Text Churn:** Authentic drafting is characterized by high text churn—students type a sentence, delete half of it, retype it with different phrasing, move paragraphs, backspace over typos, and pause to review their progress. A typical 1,000-word authentic essay requires between 2,500 and 6,000 total keystroke events.

### The Telemetric Signature of Tier 2 Grammar Tool Acceptance

When a student writes an authentic essay and subsequently uses a Tier 2 grammar assistant (like Grammarly Basic or MS Word Editor) to polish their prose, their telemetric profile remains overwhelmingly human:

* **Extensive Pre-Existing Drafting History:** The telemetric timeline reveals hours of authentic drafting, typing bursts, deletions, and structural reorganizations preceding any grammar tool interaction.
* **Localized Insertion Indices:** When the student accepts a grammar suggestion, the telemetry records a localized cursor jump to a specific character offset, a brief replacement of 1 to 4 words (e.g., replacing *"effect"* with *"affect"*), and an immediate return to reading or revising.
* **Persistent Low-Level IKI Cadence:** The surrounding text retains its original, human-generated temporal fingerprint.

## 9. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Q1: How do we handle students whose laptops have Grammarly or Apple Intelligence enabled by default at the OS level?
Writing Program Administrators should adopt policies that focus on **functional impact rather than binary software bans**. Operating-system level tools (such as Apple Intelligence or Windows Copilot) and browser extensions (Grammarly) provide both Tier 1/2 proofreading and Tier 3/5 generative features. WPAs should communicate to students that utilizing OS tools for spelling, punctuation, and word choice suggestions (**Tier 1 & Tier 2**) is fully authorized. However, clicking generative features (such as *"Rewrite Paragraph"*, *"Summarize"*, or *"Generate Text"*) cross into Tier 3 and Tier 5. Checkmark's **Essay Playback™** and **Paste Buffer Inspector** clearly distinguish between discrete word corrections and wholesale text injections, ensuring students are never penalized for having background OS utilities active.

### Q2: Why do traditional AI detectors flag essays where students only used Grammarly for basic punctuation and clarity?
Traditional, commercial AI detectors (like Turnitin, GPTZero, and CopyLeaks) operate strictly on **static text statistics**—namely **perplexity** (lexical predictability) and **burstiness** (sentence length variation). When a student writes an authentic essay and accepts 10 or 15 standard grammar suggestions, the software replaces idiosyncratic human phrasing with conventional academic syntax. This flattens the document's perplexity and burstiness, causing statistical classifiers to misinterpret the polished text as large language model output. Checkmark solves this problem by bypassing static statistical guesswork and analyzing the **temporal keystroke telemetry** of how the text was physically composed.

### Q3: Can a student fake their Essay Playback™ telemetry by slowly retyping an AI-generated essay from a phone or second screen?
No. Retyping text from an external source (transcription) produces a completely different telemetric fingerprint than authentic human composition. When a student transcribes text, their **Inter-Key Interval (IKI)** is remarkably uniform (isochronous), their cognitive pauses at clause boundaries disappear, and their revision/backspace churn rate drops to nearly zero (<2%). Checkmark's **Essay Playback™** telemetry engine easily detects this mechanical transcription pattern, flagging the absence of natural human ideation hesitations and extensive text churn.

### Q4: What is the difference between Tier 3 structural rewriting and legitimate peer editing or Writing Center tutoring?
The critical distinction lies in **metacognitive agency and dialogue**. When a student works with a peer tutor or Writing Center consultant, the tutor asks guiding questions: *"What point are you trying to make here? How might you connect these two ideas?"* The student must articulate their thoughts and manually compose the revised sentence, deepening their rhetorical awareness. In contrast, Tier 3 automated rewriters (e.g., QuillBot, GrammarlyGO) execute instantaneous syntactic replacement without requiring the student to understand the grammatical or rhetorical mechanics of the change, bypassing the cognitive learning process.

### Q5: How should WPAs train adjunct and contingent faculty to maintain consistent policy enforcement without burning out?
WPAs should provide adjunct instructors with **pre-configured Canvas LMS / Buzz LMS course shells** equipped with Checkmark's automated workflows. By utilizing Checkmark's **AI-Powered Rubric Autograder**, adjuncts receive pre-scored rubric criteria with quote-anchored justifications, dramatically reducing grading fatigue. Furthermore, WPAs should establish a clear departmental protocol: instructors are never required to spend hours investigating disputed drafts. If an integrity question arises, the instructor simply opens Checkmark's Essay Playback™ video timeline—resolving the inquiry in under 3 minutes.

### Q6: Does Checkmark Plagiarism store student essays to train future AI models or share with third parties?
**Absolutely not.** Checkmark operates under a strict **Zero-Data Training Policy**. Student submissions, keystroke telemetry, and grading histories are strictly protected under FERPA and COPPA guidelines. Student data is encrypted in transit and at rest, is never indexed into public LLM training datasets, and is never sold or shared with third-party vendors. The institution retains 100% ownership and control of all student intellectual property.

### Q7: How does Checkmark pass rubric grades and telemetry links directly back into Canvas SpeedGrader and Buzz LMS?
Checkmark utilizes advanced **LTI 1.3 Advantage Protocols** and REST API integrations. When an instructor reviews and approves an essay's rubric scores in Checkmark, the platform automatically pushes the finalized criterion scores, overall point totals, quote-anchored written feedback, and a secure direct link to the Essay Playback™ report directly into Canvas SpeedGrader, Buzz LMS, or Google Classroom gradebooks with a single click. Instructors never have to manually re-enter grades across multiple windows.
