### 1.1 The Accreditation Mandate: Demonstrating Equivalent Rigor Under HLC, SACSCOC, and WASC

Regional accrediting bodies establish strict mandates governing dual and concurrent enrollment programs. Under guidelines established by the **Higher Learning Commission (Criterion 3.A & 4.A)**, the **Southern Association of Colleges and Schools Commission on Colleges (SACSCOC Standard 10.7)**, and the **WASC Senior College and University Commission (WSCUC)**, postsecondary institutions must guarantee that off-campus and dual-credit offerings maintain identical learning outcomes, academic standards, and grading rigor as on-campus sections taught by full-time university faculty.

In First-Year Composition, this standard centers on **the writing portfolio**—a cumulative collection of multi-draft argumentative research papers, rhetorical analyses, and synthesis essays. College English Department Chairs are legally and administratively responsible for certifying that every portfolio submitted for college credit represents the **independent, unassisted intellectual labor** of the enrolled student.

When dual enrollment courses are delivered asynchronously or within distant high school classrooms, college administrators have historically relied on final paper submissions. In the era of Large Language Models (LLMs), however, receiving 4-year institutions and university curriculum committees increasingly view unverified off-campus portfolios with intense skepticism. If a university cannot verify that a student independently conducted the synthesis, drafted the prose, and executed substantive revisions, the institution faces accreditation sanctions or risks losing state transfer articulation agreements.

### 1.2 The Vulnerability of Transfer Credit Articulation

The core promise of dual enrollment is **transferability**. When a high school senior earns an "A" in a concurrent ENGL 101 course sponsored by a local community college, they expect that credit to transfer seamlessly to flagship state universities or private 4-year institutions.

However, higher education articulation officers are establishing stricter portfolio audit thresholds:

1. **Portfolio Audits on Transfer Admission:** An increasing number of receiving universities require transferring high school students to submit their complete FYC portfolios for departmental review before granting general education English equivalency.
2. **Post-Submission AI Screening:** When receiving departments run incoming portfolios through standalone AI detectors, the presence of an arbitrary probabilistic flag (e.g., *"68% Probability of AI Generation"*) frequently triggers an immediate denial of transfer credit, forcing the student to retake First-Year Composition at full university tuition.
3. **The Absence of Defense Records:** When students appeal these transfer rejections, neither the high school nor the sponsoring community college possesses an empirical audit trail to substantiate how the student created the text over time.

### 1.3 The Asynchronous High School Environment

Dual enrollment writing occurs in a fundamentally fragmented technological landscape:

* **1:1 School Laptops vs. Home Devices:** Students alternate between school-issued Chromebooks, home desktop computers, and mobile tablets.
* **Offline Drafting & Patchwork Workflows:** Students compose fragments during study halls, download notes in library computer labs, and assemble research papers across irregular schedules.
* **LMS Disconnects:** Drafts are often shared via Google Classroom or school-district portals that do not federate telemetry back into the collegiate learning management system (such as Canvas, Blackboard, or Brightspace).

When an essay is downloaded as a static `.docx` or exported as a `.pdf` for final portfolio submission, **100% of the cognitive drafting context is erased**. The final document contains words on a page, but zero forensic metadata documenting the hours of research, struggling pauses, false starts, structural overhauls, and intellectual effort that produced those words.

### 3.1 Signature 1: Temporal Drafting Session Volume & Longitudinal Spacing

Authentic collegiate writing does not materialize instantaneously. First-Year Composition essays (typically 1,500 to 3,000 words) require extensive cognitive labor distributed across multiple working sessions.

#### A. Longitudinal Distribution Across Calendar Days
In authentic student drafting, writing telemetry reveals a multi-session timeline. A student will spend Session 1 formulating an outline and drafting the introduction, return two days later in Session 2 to draft body arguments, and conduct Sessions 3 and 4 over the subsequent weekend to synthesize secondary research sources and refine transitions.

#### B. Active Drafting Duration vs. Idle Time
Telemetry systems like Checkmark distinguish between **active composing time** (active keystroke entry, cursor manipulation, and active editing) and **idle document time** (when a tab is left open in the background while the student is away). For a 2,000-word collegiate research essay, authentic independent composition typically reflects between **4 to 12 aggregate hours of active composing telemetry**.

#### C. Circadian Spacing
Telemetry records the timestamp of each session, verifying that the work reflects realistic human sleep and activity rhythms rather than instantaneous middle-of-the-night automated document generation.

Telemetry monitors and visualizes these **macro-structural revision bursts**:

1. **Global vs. Surface Revisions:** Surface revisions consist merely of spelling corrections or single-word synonym replacements. 2. **Deletions and Substitution Volume:** Authentic First-Year Composition drafting exhibits a healthy ratio of **deletions to gross character insertions** (typically 15% to 35% deletion volume). ## 8. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### How does writing telemetry differentiate between legitimate spell-check corrections and AI rewriting?
Legitimate spell-check or grammar tools (such as native browser spell-checkers or Grammarly basic) generate localized, word-level or phrase-level character substitutions within an existing sentence structure. These edits leave a distinct telemetry trail of localized cursor positioning and brief pause intervals. In contrast, generative AI rewriting tools (or LLM prompt pasting) replace entire sentences, paragraphs, or multi-paragraph blocks instantaneously through external clipboard insertion without preceding drafting keystrokes.

### What if a dual-enrollment student drafts their essay offline without an internet connection?
Checkmark’s client-side extensions and embedded editor tooling store keystroke telemetry, timing intervals, and editing events in an encrypted, local offline cache. When the student reconnects to the school or home Wi-Fi network, the complete chronological telemetry batch synchronizes seamlessly to the cloud dossier, ensuring offline drafting is 100% verified.

### Can students bypass writing telemetry by using dictation or Speech-to-Text software?
No. Speech-to-text (STT) assistive software inputs character strings in distinct phonetic chunks corresponding to spoken breath groups and voice recognition buffer flushes (typically 3–12 words per burst, accompanied by specific operating system accessibility flags). Checkmark’s telemetry engine natively recognizes assistive dictation patterns and differentiates them from unauthorized external text pastes or continuous mechanical transcription.

### How does Checkmark protect student privacy across high school and college LMS portals under FERPA?
Under **34 CFR § 99.5(a)(1)**, FERPA rights transfer to the student when enrolling in postsecondary courses, while parents maintain inspection rights over K-12 secondary records. Checkmark solves this dual-status paradox by deploying role-based access control (RBAC) via **1EdTech LTI 1.3 Advantage**. High school instructors, college department chairs, and students access verified dossiers within their respective authorized institutional LMS environments without exposing student data to unauthorized third parties or commercial LLMs.

### What minimum drafting time should college English departments require for a First-Year Composition essay?
While typing speeds and cognitive drafting styles vary across individual writers, higher education composition research indicates that an authentic 1,500-to-2,500-word collegiate research essay requires a baseline of **3.5 to 8 active composing hours** distributed across at least 2 to 4 distinct writing sessions. Checkmark allows departments to establish customized formative telemetry thresholds tailored to course objectives.

### How does writing telemetry support neurodivergent writers (e.g., students with ADHD or Autism)?
Neurodivergent students frequently compose non-linearly—jumping between sections, producing rapid bursts of text followed by prolonged executive functioning pauses, or deleting and rewriting large passages. While standalone probabilistic AI classifiers frequently misidentify neurodivergent writing patterns as machine-generated text, Checkmark’s writing telemetry captures the authentic cognitive effort, non-linear cursor movements, and organic keystrokes, providing objective proof that exonerates neurodivergent writers.

### How does Checkmark assist high schools and colleges during regional accreditation audits (e.g., SACSCOC or HLC)?
During accreditation reviews, peer evaluation teams evaluate whether off-campus dual-credit courses maintain parity with on-campus offerings. Checkmark enables department chairs to export anonymized, aggregate telemetry reports demonstrating cross-campus drafting session volume, macro-revision depth, and standardized rubric grading consistency across all dual-credit partner high schools.
