## 1. The Assessment Crisis in Modern World Language Classrooms

For decades, foreign language educators evaluated written proficiency through structured output: short paragraph responses in Level I/II, reflective narrative journals in Level III/IV, and timed cultural comparison or argumentative synthesis essays in Advanced Placement (AP) and International Baccalaureate (IB) courses. Writing served as the primary window into a student's **interlanguage**—the dynamic, evolving linguistic system that bridges their native tongue (L1) and their developing target language (L2).

The mainstream adoption of high-accuracy Neural Machine Translation (NMT) and Large Language Models (LLMs) has fundamentally severed the link between the final written artifact and the student’s actual linguistic competence.

### 1.1 The Cognitive Collapse of Bypassing the Output Hypothesis

In applied linguistics, **Merrill Swain’s Output Hypothesis** establishes that second language acquisition does not occur through passive input alone; learners must be pushed to produce spoken or written language. The cognitive struggle of formulating a sentence forces learners to:
1. **Notice the Gap:** Recognize what they want to communicate versus what linguistic resources they currently possess.
2. **Test Hypotheses:** Experiment with grammatical structures, verb endings, and word order.
3. **Engage in Metalinguistic Reflection:** Consciously evaluate structural rules (e.g., deciding between the *Passé Composé* and *Imparfait* in French, or navigating *Por* vs. *Para* and the subjunctive mood in Spanish).

When a student delegates sentence formulation to DeepL, this vital cognitive feedback loop is completely bypassed. The student produces a final document showcasing advanced subjunctive clauses, idiomatic colloquialisms, and flawless adjective agreement, yet their neurological interlanguage remains completely stagnant.

### 1.2 The "Translation Trap" in Secondary and Higher Education

Surveys of high school foreign language departments reveal that machine translation is the single most widespread form of unapproved digital assistance:
- **78% of secondary language teachers** report encountering weekly submissions featuring vocabulary and grammar far exceeding assigned curricular levels.
- **Over 65% of students** who use machine translation do not view it as "cheating" in the traditional sense, conflating full-sentence neural generation with looking up an isolated word in a bilingual dictionary.
- **AP and IB language teachers** face acute grading distortions when students submit near-native compositions on homework assignments but stall completely during timed, in-class interpersonal speaking and handwriting assessments.

To resolve this crisis, educators must move beyond evaluating static text and examine the **temporal writing process** through which that text was produced.

### 2.1 The Mathematics of the False Positive: Low Perplexity & Low Burstiness

Statistical AI detectors evaluate text using two primary mathematical metrics:
1. **Perplexity (Word Predictability):** A measure of how likely a language model is to predict the next word in a sequence. High perplexity indicates unexpected, creative word choices; low perplexity indicates highly predictable sequences.
2. **Burstiness (Sentence Variation):** A measure of structural diversity across sentence lengths, clause complexities, and cadence. Human native writers alternate between short punchy sentences and long, complex subordinate clauses (high burstiness).

Developing language students operate with strict lexical constraints. A Spanish II student writing about their daily routine will write:
> *"Me despierto a las siete. Me cepillo los dientes. Luego, desayuno huevos con pan. Voy a la escuela en autobús."*

Because this text uses introductory grammar patterns with uniform subject-verb-object structures, an AI detector calculates **extremely low perplexity** and **near-zero burstiness**. As a result, generic AI detectors routinely label authentic, hard-working novice and intermediate language students as AI frauds, causing severe emotional distress and destroying classroom rapport.

### 2.2 The False Negative: Why DeepL Slips Under the Radar

Neural Machine Translation engines like DeepL use specialized transformer models trained on billions of aligned bilingual professional translations, literary texts, and international diplomatic proceedings. When DeepL translates an English paragraph into French, Spanish, or German, it outputs:
- Natural, idiomatic turn-of-phrase expressions rather than literal word-by-word substitutions.
- Complex syntactic inversions and varied subordinate conjunctions.
- Sophisticated vocabulary with high natural perplexity.

Because DeepL is not generating conversational padding (such as *"As an AI language model..."* or formulaic bulleted structures), its output exhibits the statistical hallmarks of an educated native speaker. Statistical AI detectors inspect the text, find high lexical richness, and output a **0% AI probability score**—completely missing a 300-word unauthorized translation paste.

1. **Depressed Typing Velocity (12–25 Words Per Minute):** Even students who type 60+ WPM in English experience a massive drop in typing speed when composing in Spanish, French, German, Chinese, or Japanese. 4. **Non-Linear Revision Cycles:** Students regularly navigate their cursor back into previously typed sentences to adjust pronouns, insert missing prepositions, or add missing accent marks and umlauts.
