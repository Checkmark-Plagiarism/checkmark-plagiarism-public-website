### The Mechanism of False Accusations Against Disabled Students

Why are students with Individualized Education Programs (IEPs) and 504 Accommodation Plans disproportionately targeted by commercial AI detectors? The failure stems from fundamental architectural flaws in how legacy detection systems operate:

1. **The Speech-to-Text (STT) "Burst Injection" Trap:** When a student with severe dysgraphia or motor apraxia speaks into voice dictation software (such as Apple Dictation, Google Voice Typing, or Dragon NaturallySpeaking), the operating system's speech recognition engine processes the acoustic stream in memory and delivers the recognized sentence into the document in a rapid 50-to-100-word buffer. Coarse document revision checkers (such as Google Docs standard version history) do not log the micro-pauses of speech; they record an instantaneous insertion of a complete paragraph. To a generic revision scanner, this looks completely indistinguishable from a user pasting a paragraph from ChatGPT.
2. **Constrained Perplexity from Word Prediction Engines:** Word prediction and assistive vocabulary software (e.g., Co:Writer, Read&Write, WordQ) assist students with specific learning disabilities by predicting upcoming words based on initial phonemes and semantic context. By design, these tools suggest grammatically standard, highly probable lexical choices. When an autistic student or a student with an executive functioning disability relies on word prediction, the resulting prose exhibits uniformly high statistical predictability (low perplexity)—the exact statistical signature that commercial AI detectors use to classify text as synthetic.
3. **Non-Linear Drafting and "Bricolage" Assembly:** Students with ADHD, executive dysfunction, or traumatic brain injuries rarely compose essays linearly from Introduction to Conclusion. They compose in modular chunks—brainstorming bullet points in personal digital scratchpads (Apple Notes, Google Keep, Notion), composing the evidentiary counterargument first, and copying structural outlines into the LMS submission portal. Static detectors and naive diff algorithms misread these non-linear movements as disingenuous assembly.
4. **Phonological Correction Scaffolds:** Grammar and spell-check engines tailored for special education (such as assistive phonetic checkers) automatically normalize unconventional syntax. Legacy AI detectors interpret this polished surface fluency as machine-generated output, penalizing the student for the exact accommodation granted to level their educational playing field.

### 1. Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA, 20 U.S.C. § 1400 et seq.)

Under IDEA, public school districts are legally obligated to provide every eligible child with a disability a **Free Appropriate Public Education (FAPE)** tailored through an **Individualized Education Program (IEP)**.
* **Assistive Technology Mandate (34 CFR § 300.105):** The IEP team must consider whether the child requires assistive technology devices and services to access the general education curriculum. When an IEP specifies accommodations such as *"Use of Speech-to-Text software for all written assignments exceeding one paragraph"* or *"Access to word prediction software (Co:Writer) during drafting,"* this is a **federally binding legal contract**.
* **Denial of FAPE via Algorithmic Penalization:** If a general education instructor assigns a failing grade, issues a disciplinary referral, or forces a student to rewrite an assignment because an uncalibrated commercial AI detector flagged the student's voice-dictated text, the school district has committed a substantive denial of FAPE. 
* **Manifestation Determination Review (MDR) Requirements (34 CFR § 300.530(e)):** Prior to imposing disciplinary sanctions (such as suspension, loss of credit, or removal from academic honors programs) that result in a change of placement, the district must convene an MDR to determine if the alleged conduct was a direct manifestation of the student's disability or the direct result of the district's failure to implement the IEP. Using an unverified AI score to bypass this process violates federal law.

### 2. Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 (29 U.S.C. § 794)

Section 504 protects students with physical or mental impairments that substantially limit one or more major life activities (including learning, reading, concentrating, thinking, communicating, and manual tasks).
* **Non-Discrimination & Equal Access (34 CFR § 104.4):** Recipients of federal financial assistance may not afford a qualified individual with a disability an opportunity to participate in or benefit from aids, benefits, or services that is not equal to that afforded others.
* **Disparate Impact of Black-Box Detection Tools:** Deploying an academic integrity scanner that systematically flags assistive technology output at rates dramatically higher than standard neuro-typical typing creates an unlawful **disparate impact**. The district cannot defend the use of a software tool that effectively treats approved accommodations as evidence of guilt.

### 3. Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA, 42 U.S.C. § 12131 et seq.)

Title II extends non-discrimination protections across all public entities, including state colleges, universities, and public school systems.
* **Auxiliary Aids and Services (28 CFR § 35.160):** Public entities must furnish appropriate auxiliary aids and services—including speech-recognition software, text-to-speech engines, and specialized input hardware—where necessary to afford individuals with disabilities an equal opportunity to participate in academic programs.
* **Prohibition of Surcharges or Administrative Burdens:** Imposing disproportionate interrogation, administrative scrutiny, or evidentiary burdens on students solely because they use auxiliary aids constitutes unlawful discrimination.

## 8. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

### 1. Why do generic commercial AI detectors flag speech-to-text (STT) dictation as AI-generated writing?
Commercial AI detectors evaluate text based on two statistical properties: **perplexity** (word choice predictability) and **burstiness** (sentence structure and length variation). When students use speech-to-text dictation, they tend to speak in complete, grammatically standard clauses with consistent cadence, producing text with lower lexical perplexity than raw, unassisted student typing. Furthermore, because speech recognition engines deliver transcribed sentences in rapid API data packets (e.g., 20 words appearing in 150 milliseconds), naive document revision checkers misclassify these voice bursts as copy-pasted LLM text. Checkmark eliminates this failure by analyzing the microsecond-level acoustic pause topography, vocalized punctuation tokens, and subsequent phonological edits that uniquely characterize human voice composition.
