## 1. The Disciplinary Dilemma: Generative AI in the Business Case Classroom

For more than a century, collegiate business schools and MBA programs have relied on the **case study method** to simulate real-world executive dilemmas. Students are placed in the seat of a Chief Executive Officer, Private Equity Principal, Chief Financial Officer, or Management Consultant tasked with evaluating incomplete information, reconciling conflicting financial statements, projecting future cash flows under severe uncertainty, and articulating a defensible strategic plan.

The efficacy of this pedagogy relies on an unbroken chain of reasoning connecting three distinct artifacts:
1. **Raw Quantitative Exhibit Data:** Historical financial statements (Income Statements, Balance Sheets, Cash Flow Statements), 10-K regulatory disclosures, debt covenants, and capital expenditure schedules provided in the case text.
2. **Mathematical Modeling:** Excel-based DCF valuations, Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) calculations, Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) beta unlevering/relevering, Comparable Company Analysis (Trading Multiples), and Precedent Transaction benchmarks.
3. **Strategic Narrative Synthesis:** An executive memorandum justifying capital allocation, M&A bidding strategies, market entry, operational turnaround, or organizational restructuring based on rigorous qualitative frameworks (SWOT, Porter's Five Forces, PESTEL, BCG Growth-Share Matrix).

The resulting output presents an alarming pedagogical challenge for business faculty: **The Illusion of Executive Articulacy**. 

Because LLMs are trained on billions of tokens of corporate annual reports, management consulting decks (McKinsey, Boston Consulting Group, Bain), Wall Street equity research reports, and academic business journals, their prose reads with flawless executive polish. However, because LLMs generate text through statistical token prediction rather than deterministic mathematical computation, they routinely generate **fabricated financial metrics**, apply **impossible margin assumptions**, cite **non-existent balance sheet reserves**, and produce **detached strategic recommendations** that directly contradict the binding operational constraints described in the case exhibits.

### Threat Vector A: Uncredited Case Solutions and Instructor Teaching Notes
For decades, classic business cases (such as *Ben & Jerry's Homemade*, *Blaine Kitchenware*, *Midland Energy*, or *Netflix in 2011*) have had unauthorized student solutions, Excel valuation models, and instructor teaching notes leaked across the internet. Students accessing these files bypass the cognitive struggle of building financial projections from scratch, pasting pre-calculated WACC rates (e.g., $8.42\%$) or valuation conclusions directly into their papers. 

Legacy text-matching plagiarism engines can often detect these submissions if the text is copied verbatim. However, modern students routinely use AI paraphrasers (e.g., QuillBot or Undetectable AI) to rewrite the leaked solution manual text, evading traditional keyword and phrase-matching filters entirely.

### Threat Vector B: AI-Synthesized Hallucinations and Fabricated Metrics
When students prompt an LLM to generate a business case analysis from scratch or provide it with raw case excerpts, the AI frequently confabulates data to satisfy the prompt's logical structure. These hallucinations fall into four distinct categories:

1. **Phantom Financial Metrics:** The AI cites specific dollar amounts, percentages, or ratios that do not appear anywhere in the case text, balance sheets, or accompanying exhibits. For example, an AI generating an analysis of an airline turnaround case may state: *"Exhibit 3 demonstrates that fuel hedging contracts locked in $68\% of crude requirements at $72/barrel,"* when Exhibit 3 actually details fleet maintenance depreciation schedules and makes no mention of fuel hedging.
2. **Sector-Incongruent Margin Structures:** LLMs frequently blend financial knowledge across unrelated corporate datasets. An essay analyzing an asset-heavy manufacturing firm or brick-and-mortar retailer might project an $88\%$ Gross Margin and a $45\%$ Free Cash Flow conversion rate—metrics typical of enterprise SaaS software companies but economically absurd for industrial fabrication.
3. **Violated Debt Covenants and Capital Constraints:** An AI memo will confidently recommend issuing $\$150	ext{M}$ in high-yield mezzanine debt to fund an immediate dividend recapitalization, completely oblivious to a prominent contractual clause in the case stating that existing senior debt covenants prohibit total debt-to-EBITDA leverage from exceeding $2.5	ext{x}$ or cash dividends from being distributed prior to fiscal year 2028.
4. **Hollow, Universal Strategic Frameworks:** When asked to provide a Porter's Five Forces or SWOT analysis, the AI generates generic industry commentary (*"Supplier power is moderate due to multiple global vendors; threat of substitutes is high due to rapid technological evolution"*) without tying a single point to the unique corporate leadership, patented technologies, union negotiations, or regulatory investigations detailed in the assigned case.

### The Corporate Jargon Paradox
Business communication is intentionally formal, concise, and structured around standardized executive vocabulary. Phrases such as:
- *"Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)"*
- *"Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization (EBITDA)"*
- *"Sustainable competitive advantage"*
- *"First-mover advantage in fragmented markets"*
- *"Synergistic cost rationalization across redundant business units"*
- *"Working capital optimization through Days Sales Outstanding (DSO) compression"*

These phrases possess inherently **low perplexity** (high statistical predictability) and **uniform burstiness** (standardized sentence lengths). When a diligent human student crafts an executive memo adhering strictly to the *Minto Pyramid Principle* or standard management consulting memo guidelines, **generic AI percentage detectors frequently flag the paper as 70%–95% AI-generated**. 

This creates severe institutional risk: business school honor committees are inundated with false accusations against top-performing students who merely mastered professional business writing conventions.

### Rewarding Style Over Exhibit Grounding
In large undergraduate or MBA cohorts (where a single professor or teaching assistant may evaluate 60 to 150 case memos per week), graders are easily charmed by articulate prose, crisp executive formatting, and bold subheadings. An AI submission that presents a beautifully structured 4-step strategic roadmap with flawless grammar often receives higher initial marks than a messy, authentic student draft that struggled through complex spreadsheet calculations but ultimately grounded its conclusions in real exhibit data.

Without tools to audit the **connection between the writing process and the underlying spreadsheet exhibits**, business faculty risk inadvertently penalizing honest, hard-working students while rewarding superficial AI-generated corporate fluff.

Before analyzing writing style, conduct a rapid **Ground-Truth Data Audit** by comparing the quantitative figures in the student's submission against the official case exhibits:

1. **Balance Sheet & Cash Flow Reconciliation:** Check whether the cited cash balances, accounts receivable, debt obligations, and capital expenditures match the case tables. If the student cites a *"$\$45	ext{M}$ liquidity reserve"* but the case balance sheet shows only $\$4.5	ext{M}$ in unrestricted cash, this is a prime indicator of an ungrounded LLM hallucination.

3. **Footnote & Exhibit Title Verification:** LLMs frequently invent exhibit names (e.g., citing *"Exhibit 7: Competitor Market Share Breakdown"* when the assigned case only contains Exhibits 1 through 5).
