## 1. The Evidence Crisis in Competitive Speech & Debate

Student debaters analyze complex public policy, macroeconomics, international relations, constitutional law, and moral philosophy.

#### Archetype 1: Card Clipping & Oral Truncation
In Policy (CX) and Lincoln-Douglas debate, competitors utilize rapid delivery (spreading, often exceeding 300 to 400 words per minute) to maximize argumentative volume. Under extreme time constraints, a debater may mark a card as containing 150 words of underlined text but prematurely cease reading at word 75 when the speech timer expires—without verbally announcing or physically marking their stop point on the shared speech document (speech drop or email chain). The team then proceeds to extend the unread back-half of the card in later rebuttal speeches, claiming an offensive warrant that was never actually read into the official round record.

#### Archetype 2: Context Stripping & Qualifier Omission
To generate an absolute link or impact (e.g., claiming a minor trade tariff will "guarantee global nuclear conflict" or "cause immediate economic collapse"), students selectively highlight fragments while omitting adjacent conditional clauses. When an author writes:
> *"While it is theoretically conceivable under extreme hypothetical parameters that tariff escalation could disrupt supply chains, empirical data demonstrates that modern central banks routinely absorb such shocks without triggering recessionary collapse."*

A student engaged in context stripping highlights only:
> *"...tariff escalation could disrupt supply chains... triggering recessionary collapse."*

By stripping the author's governing thesis and empirical caveat, the student has committed a severe contextual distortion—transforming a scholarly refutation of economic catastrophe into purported proof of its inevitability.

#### Archetype 3: Bracket & Punctuation Manipulation
Under NSDA evidence guidelines, brackets `[...]` are strictly reserved for grammatical clarifiers (e.g., substituting "[the European Union]" for "they" when reading an excerpted paragraph). However, unethical card cutters frequently use brackets to insert substantive argumentative warrants that the original author never penned. Similarly, stripping question marks (e.g., changing *"Can AI replace human judges?"* to *"AI [will] replace human judges"*) creates artificial certainty from exploratory inquiries.

#### Archetype 4: AI-Generated Phantom Citations (The 2026 LLM Wave)
With the proliferation of generative large language models, a dangerous new failure mode has entered high school debate: **hallucinated scholarship**. When debaters run out of time during prep blocks, some prompt LLMs to "write an affirmative card proving that modular nuclear reactors reduce grid volatility with an academic citation." LLMs routinely generate plausible-sounding titles, authoritative-sounding author names (e.g., "Dr. Marcus Sterling, MIT Energy Initiative, 2025"), and convincing statistics that correspond to no real-world publication. When challenged in cross-examination, students often cannot produce the underlying PDF or URL because the evidence is entirely synthetic.
