### The Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) Under Threat

In second-language acquisition theory (Vygotsky, 1978; Krashen, 1982; Swain, 1985), language competence develops through **comprehensible output** and the cognitive struggle of encoding complex thought into a developing lexical and morphosyntactic framework. When a bilingual 10th grader writing an essay on Isabel Allende's *La casa de los espíritus* struggles to choose between the preterite (*escribió*) and the imperfect (*escribía*), or wrestles with subjunctive clause embedding (*dudo que sea verdad*), that cognitive struggle is not an obstacle to learning—**it is the learning itself**.

When students utilize generative AI or neural machine translation to bypass this struggle:
1. **Linguistic Fossilization Occurs:** Students fail to develop target-language mental schemas, remaining permanently dependent on digital intermediaries.
2. **Authentic Translanguaging Is Stifled:** Teachers cannot diagnose authentic developmental errors (such as interlanguage calques, overgeneralization of irregular verbs, or false cognates) because the submitted prose reflects the algorithmic perfection of a multi-billion-parameter neural network.
3. **Assessment Validity Collapses:** Standards-based assessments tied to the **ACTFL (American Council on the Teaching of Foreign Languages) Proficiency Guidelines** or **WIDA English Language Development Standards** become completely decoupled from actual student output.

### The Double-Bind of Commercial AI Detectors

When DLI educators attempt to address this challenge using legacy, whole-document statistical AI detectors, they trigger a catastrophic equity crisis. 

Commercial AI detectors rely primarily on two statistical metrics:
* **Perplexity ($PPL$):** A measure of how surprised an autoregressive language model is by a sequence of words. Low perplexity indicates highly predictable, conventional word choices.
* **Burstiness ($B$):** The variance in sentence length, rhythm, and structural complexity across a document.

Because emerging bilingual writers rely on standardized vocabulary sets, predictable clause templates, and formal transitional markers (*En primer lugar*, *Par conséquent*, *综上所述*, *Furthermore*), their authentic writing naturally exhibits **low perplexity and low burstiness**. Consequently, statistical detectors systematically flag genuine non-native writing as AI-generated.

## 2. Deconstructing the Cognitive Psycholinguistics of Multilingual Writing

To understand why keystroke telemetry succeeds where static text analysis fails, educators must examine the psycholinguistic mechanics of bilingual writing versus machine generation.

### 2.1 The "False-Positive Vulnerability": Why Static Classifiers Fail Emerging Bilinguals

In 2023, a landmark study by researchers at Stanford University (Liang et al., *"GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers"*) tested seven leading commercial AI detection engines on human-written essays. While native English essays were classified with over 97% accuracy, the exact same detectors misclassified **61.3% to 97.8% of authentic non-native human essays (TOEFL submissions) as AI-generated**. More than 20% of authentic bilingual essays were flagged as synthetic by every single commercial engine tested.

The mathematical reason is straightforward:

$$\text{Perplexity}(W) = \exp\left( -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \ln P(w_i \mid w_1, \dots, w_{i-1}) \right)$$

When an emerging bilingual student writes in $L_2$, their working memory relies on high-frequency target vocabulary and direct grammatical templates taught in class. Because their word selections align almost perfectly with the most probable statistical paths of language models, statistical detectors conclude that the text was generated by an LLM. 

Conversely, when a student takes an AI-generated essay and runs it through a paraphrasing tool like QuillBot, the tool deliberately substitutes lower-frequency synonyms and alters clause boundaries. This artificially elevates the perplexity and burstiness, allowing completely outsourced writing to glide past commercial detectors with a "0% AI" score.

#### 1. Lexical Retrieval Pauses (LRP)
When composing in an acquired language, bilingual working memory must access target-language lemmas, apply morphosyntactic inflection rules (gender agreement, case endings, verb conjugations), and inhibit $L_1$ lexical interference. 

In writing telemetry, this manifests as **prolonged intra-clause hesitation pauses lasting between 5 and 18 seconds**. Unlike native writers, whose pauses cluster heavily at major paragraph and sentence boundaries, bilingual writers pause *inside* clauses—immediately preceding low-frequency nouns, complex verb conjugations, or relative pronouns.

#### 2. Cross-Linguistic Keystroke Friction Ratio ($KFR = 18\% - 35\%$)
Authentic drafting is inherently non-linear and messy. Checkmark Plagiarism quantifies this composing struggle via the **Keystroke Friction Ratio ($KFR$)**:

$$KFR = \left( \frac{\text{Backspace Keystrokes} + \text{Delete Keystrokes} + \text{Cursor Navigation Jumps} + \text{Inline Edits}}{\text{Total Keystrokes Executed}} \right) \times 100$$

For authentic emerging bilingual writers in Dual-Language Immersion settings, empirical telemetry demonstrates that the $KFR$ consistently falls within the **18% to 35% range**. 

Students continuously engage in self-repair:
* Correcting gender agreement (e.g., typing *el problema*, then backspacing to correct an accidental *la problema*).
* Cycling through verb conjugations (e.g., in French: typing *ils mangeaient*, deleting to *ils ont mangé*, then re-evaluating the historical timeframe).
* Resolving syntactic word-order calques (e.g., moving an English adjective-noun sequence *la blanca casa* to the correct Spanish postposition *la casa blanca*).

#### 3. Intermittent Permissible Ingestion
Authentic bilingual writers frequently consult approved reference tools (e.g., WordReference, Real Academia Española [RAE], Le Robert, Collins, Pleco). 

In writing process telemetry, this appears as **intermittent micro-pastes of 1 to 3 words** (rarely exceeding 5 words). Crucially, the student immediately resumes manual typing within 1–3 seconds, embedding the looked-up vocabulary item into the surrounding syntactic framework.

#### 1. Unnatural Isochronous Typing ($CV_{IKI} < 0.18$)
When a student attempts to bypass paste-detection logs by placing an AI translation window on a second monitor, tablet, or split screen and manually retyping the text, their typing telemetry betrays them. 

Natural human drafting features high variability in **Inter-Keystroke Intervals (IKI)**: rapid bursts for familiar words followed by long pauses for ideation. Optical transcription, however, produces **isochronous (rhythmically flat) typing**:

$$CV_{IKI} = \frac{\sigma_{IKI}}{\mu_{IKI}} < 0.18$$

A Coefficient of Variation ($CV_{IKI}$) below 0.18 indicates that the student is not generating thoughts or retrieving vocabulary—they are mechanically transcribing visual characters from an external display at a steady, unvarying pace.

#### 2. Monolithic Block Pastes
The most blatant signature of machine translation is the instantaneous ingestion of multi-sentence or multi-paragraph target-language prose. A student pastes 250 to 600 words into a blank editor in a single 0.0-second event, followed by zero grammatical adjustments.

#### 3. Complete Absence of Morphosyntactic Revision ($KFR < 3\%$)
In machine-paraphrased writing, complex target-language syntactic structures—such as intricate conditional inversions in French (*Si j'avais su, je serais venu*), complex subjunctive noun clauses in Spanish (*No creo que hayan comprendido las implicaciones socioeconómicas*), or Chengyu four-character idioms in Mandarin (破釜沉舟)—appear on the screen in flawless succession without a single backspace, deletion, or hesitation pause. 

For an emerging bilingual student whose oral proficiency is at the ACTFL Intermediate-Low level, the sudden keystroke-level production of Superior-level morphosyntax with a $KFR$ below 3% is definitive proof of external cognitive outsourcing.

The Keystroke Friction Ratio ($KFR$) measures the percentage of drafting labor dedicated to revisions, deletions, backspacing, and structural navigation:

A $KFR$ below 3% in a complex text is a primary telemetric indicator of machine translation or optical transcription.
