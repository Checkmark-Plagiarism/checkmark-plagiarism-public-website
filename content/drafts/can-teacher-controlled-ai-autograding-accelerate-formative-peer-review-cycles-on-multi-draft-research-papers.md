### The Math Behind the 140-Draft Avalanche
Consider the typical workload of a secondary humanities teacher or college writing instructor:
* **Cohort Size:** 4 to 5 class sections totaling 120 to 150 students.
* **Draft Scope:** A 2,000- to 3,000-word analytical research paper synthesizing 10 to 20 secondary sources.
* **Total Word Volume:** 300,000 to 450,000 words of student writing submitted simultaneously at the Draft 1 milestone.
* **Evaluation Velocity:** Reading a complex research draft, validating in-text citations against a bibliography, mapping logical claims, and writing constructive, actionable rubric commentary requires **25 to 35 minutes per student** for even seasoned evaluators.
* **The Aggregate Deficit:** Scoring 140 drafts requires **60 to 80 hours of uninterrupted teacher labor**. 

If a teacher attempts to provide comprehensive written feedback before the scheduled peer-review workshop, drafts sit in a grading backlog for two to three weeks. By the time students receive their marked-up papers, the classroom has moved on to the next unit, the intellectual momentum is lost, and students merely accept the grade rather than engaging in authentic, exploratory revision.

### The College Board AP Capstone Regulatory Constraint
This grading bottleneck is especially severe in standardized research frameworks such as the **College Board AP Capstone Diploma Program** (AP Seminar and AP Research). Under official College Board operational rules:
> *"Teachers may provide feedback to students on their initial drafts... Once students begin the final submission process, teachers may not revise, edit, or provide line-by-line feedback on student work."*

Because educators are strictly barred from editing or line-by-line coaching on final submissions to the College Board Digital Portfolio, **the formative feedback window on rough Draft 1 is the single opportunity teachers have to steer students toward academic success.** Missing this window leaves students stranded with structural flaws that permanently depress their national exam distributions.

## 2. Anatomy of the "Superficial Peer-Review" Breakdown

### 1. The Cognitive Load Overload
Under John Sweller’s **Cognitive Load Theory**, working memory can only process a limited number of information elements simultaneously. Reading a 2,500-word multi-source research paper requires an evaluator to execute five simultaneous mental operations:
1. Decoding complex syntax and domain-specific terminology.
2. Tracking the primary thesis across multi-layered body paragraphs.
3. Evaluating whether cited evidence logically supports adjacent claims.
4. Assessing whether counterarguments are treated with substantive nuance or trivialized.
5. Monitoring surface grammar, mechanics, and citation formatting.

For novice student writers, executing all five operations concurrently causes severe cognitive overload. To preserve mental bandwidth, students instinctively retreat to the easiest, most concrete task: **hunting for mechanical errors** (spelling mistakes, punctuation errors, formatting inconsistencies). They spend 45 minutes fixing five missing commas while completely ignoring that the author’s primary line of reasoning collapses in paragraph 4.

### 2. Social Hesitancy and the "Rubber-Stamp" Dynamic
Peer dynamics in secondary and undergraduate settings are fraught with social friction. Students are acutely aware of peer hierarchies and fear appearing harsh, hypercritical, or pedantic. Consequently, unguided peer review generates the familiar "rubber-stamp" syndrome:
* *"I love your topic! Your essay is super interesting."*
* *"Everything flows really well, maybe just add a transition here."*
* *"Good job citing sources, looks ready to turn in!"*

These superficial affirmations comfort the author but provide zero actionable scaffolding for structural revision.

### 3. The Absence of Objective Diagnostic Anchors
When high school seniors or college freshmen read a peer's literature review, they rarely know how to verify whether five cited empirical studies are genuinely *synthesized* in conversation with one another or merely *strung together in isolation* ("quote-bombing"). Without an objective visual breakdown that highlights evidentiary gaps, peer reviewers lack the diagnostic confidence to challenge their peers' arguments.
