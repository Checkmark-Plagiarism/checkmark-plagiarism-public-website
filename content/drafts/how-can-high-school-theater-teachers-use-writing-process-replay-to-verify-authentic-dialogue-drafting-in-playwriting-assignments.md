### 2.1 The Craft of Dramatic Writing: Voice, Subtext, Timing, and Beats

Authentic playwriting is defined by specific technical elements that reflect real-time cognitive wrestling:

* **Character Voice Differentiation (Idiolect):** In a well-crafted play, no two characters sound alike. A world-weary grandmother speaks with different rhythmic cadence, syntactic complexity, and colloquialisms than an anxious high school freshman.
* **Subtext (The Unspoken Drama):** Characters rarely state their exact feelings directly. Real dramatic tension exists in the chasm between what a character *says* and what they *mean* or *do*.
* **Dramatic Beats & Timing:** A "beat" is the smallest unit of dramatic action where a character shifts tactics to achieve an objective. Human writers pause, deliberate, restructure, and delete lines as they calibrate the rhythm of these shifts.
* **Functional Stage Directions:** Stage directions in modern theater serve actor blocking, physical action, and theatrical tempo—not internal emotional exposition.

---

### 2.2 Linguistic Hallmarks of AI-Generated Scripts (The "LLM Theatrical Accent")

Large Language Models do not experience emotional conflict, physical space, or acoustic timing. When prompted to generate dramatic scripts, they default to predictable statistical averages, exhibiting what theater educators recognize as the **"LLM Theatrical Accent"**:

#### 1. Symmetrical Conversational Turn-Taking (The "Ping-Pong" Flaw)
LLMs generate dialogue with unnaturally balanced, polite turn-taking. Character A speaks two complete, grammatically balanced sentences; Character B responds with two complete, grammatically balanced sentences. Human conversations—especially dramatic ones—are messy, consisting of rapid one-word interjections, trailing thoughts, sudden overlaps, and non-sequiturs.

#### 2. On-the-Nose Exposition and Emotional Directness
LLMs lack the capacity for dramatic subtext. Characters in AI scripts explicitly articulate their inner emotional states:
```
// Typical AI Script Dialogue:
SARAH: I am feeling deeply hurt because you forgot my birthday, which symbolizes how distant our friendship has become since you joined the track team.
```
In contrast, a high school playwright drafting authentic subtext will compose dialogue layered with deflections:
```
// Authentic Student Dialogue:
SARAH: (Looking at her phone) Nice shoes. New?
MARCUS: Track practice.
SARAH: Right. Must take up a lot of time. Like... all day Tuesday.
```

#### 3. Flattening of Distinct Character Dialects
When prompted for regional dialects or teen slang, LLMs produce either caricature or sanitized, uniform grammar. The authentic, evolving sociolect of high school students—rich with hyper-local slang, meme references, and idiosyncratic grammar—is smoothed out into standard middle-class American prose.

#### 4. The "After-School Special" Moralizing Resolution
LLM-generated scenes almost invariably resolve in neat, therapeutic moral consensus within three to five pages. Characters achieve mutual understanding, apologize for their shortcomings, and articulate a tidy life lesson. Authentic adolescent playwriting frequently grapples with unresolved ambiguity, uncomfortable silences, and painful, messy realities.

#### 5. Redundant Parenthetical Directives
AI scripts rely heavily on redundant, amateurish emotional parentheticals before almost every dialogue line: *(angrily)*, *(thoughtfully)*, *(with deep regret)*, *(smiling warmly)*. Professional dramatic writing and rigorous high school rubrics teach students to reveal emotion through action and text, rather than spoon-feeding actors via parentheticals.

### 2.3 The Human Playwriting Signature: Messy, Non-Linear, and Phonetic

When a student authentically writes a play, their drafting process leaves an indelible, empirical signature:
* **Phonetic Dialect Calibration:** Students will type a line of dialogue, delete it, retype it phonetically to capture an accent, and adjust punctuation to indicate pauses.
* **Line Pruning and Beat Shifting:** An authentic writer will write a 10-line monologue, realize it is too wordy, cut seven lines, and insert a single stage direction: `[MARCUS sets down the letter. He doesn't look up.]`
* **Non-Linear Jumping:** Playwrights frequently jump between character responses, drafting the climax first, working backward to plant foreshadowing, and shifting blocking notes as they visualize the stage space.

### 3.1 The Chilling Effect of Black-Box Probabilistic Scoring

Theater education relies on psychological safety. Students share personal traumas, family dynamics, and social anxieties through the surrogate voices of fictional characters. 

If an English or Drama teacher submits a student's original one-act to a generic AI detector and receives an arbitrary "78% AI" flag, the teacher is forced into an adversarial posture. When accused, the student has no way to prove their innocence because traditional word processors provide no granular record of their creative labor. The result is catastrophic: honest students become cynical, disengage from theater productions, and refuse to participate in future play festivals.

### 3.2 The Flaw of Perplexity and Burstiness in Stylized Theatrical Dialogue

Standard AI detectors operate on two statistical heuristics:
* **Perplexity:** The statistical predictability of the next word in a sequence.
* **Burstiness:** The variation in sentence length and structure across a document.

These metrics fail completely when applied to dramatic scripts:
* **Theatrical Dialogue is Naturally Repetitive:** Real stage dialogue employs rhythmic repetition, refrains, comedic "rule of three" callbacks, and staccato monosyllabic responses (*"No." / "Yes." / "Never." / "Why?"*). Generic detectors calculate this as "low burstiness" and flag it as machine-generated.
* **Dramatic Pauses and Line Breaks:** Scripts contain non-standard formatting, broken sentence fragments, and isolated stage directions that distort the linguistic parsing models used by commercial essay scanners.

### 3.3 The Failure to Catch "Humanized" AI Plays

Conversely, students who use generative AI know how to evade text-only detectors. By prompting an LLM to *"write a play scene with typos and slang"* or by passing the script through an automated text-spinner, the student alters the statistical perplexity. The detector gives the plagiarized script a clean bill of health. 

The only unforgeable record of true authorship is the **chronological human drafting timeline**.

Authentic verification begins before the first line of dialogue is typed:
1. **Assign Drafting Within Connected Environments:** Require students to compose their plays inside an integrated LMS (such as Canvas LMS, Buzz LMS, or Google Classroom) or the Checkmark-enabled Google Docs / Web Editor.

### Phase 2: Telemetry Triaging (Revision Bursts vs. Mechanical Cadence)

When scripts are submitted, the drama teacher reviews the telemetry overview dashboard:
* **Total Active Time vs. Word Count:** A standard 5-page one-act script (approx. 1,200 words) typically requires 2 to 5 hours of cognitive drafting time. A total active drafting time of under 8 minutes is an immediate telemetry anomaly.
* **Keystroke-to-Word Ratio:** Authentic creative writing produces a high keystroke-to-word ratio (often 2.5:1 to 4:1) due to frequent backspacing, punctuation adjustment, and line rewrites. A 1:1 ratio (where every keystroke appears in the final document with zero deletions) indicates manual transcription rather than original composition.
* **Inter-Keystroke Interval (IKI) Variance:** Human writers display erratic typing speeds—bursting through a quick line of banter, then pausing for 45 seconds to ponder a character's reply. Monotonous, unvarying typing speeds indicate a student retyping text from a secondary screen, phone, or printed generative AI printout.

### Q2: What if a student writes their script in a spiral notebook or on paper first and then types it into the computer?
**A:** This is a common practice in creative writing. When a student types from handwritten notes, their keystroke dynamics will reflect transcription (steady typing speed with few deletions). However, in Checkmark’s restorative framework, this is not treated as cheating.
