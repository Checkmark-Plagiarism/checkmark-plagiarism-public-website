Unlike routine classroom grading conferences, formal appeal hearings function as **quasi-judicial administrative tribunals**. When a student appeals a referring instructor's allegation of plagiarism, unauthorized collaboration, or generative AI authorship fraud, the panel must weigh contradictory testimonies, analyze disputed digital artifacts, and render binding findings of fact.

In recent years, the nature of these appeals has undergone a seismic transformation. Historically, plagiarism adjudication rested upon tangible, easily verifiable evidence: an instructor identified an uncredited paragraph, retrieved the physical textbook or URL from which it was copied, and placed the two texts side by side. Today, however, academic integrity panels are inundated with ambiguous, emotionally charged disputes centered on black-box artificial intelligence classifiers (e.g., *"This paper is 86% AI-generated"*) or uninterpreted similarity scores (e.g., *"32% similarity index"*).

### The Constitutional and Contractual Due Process Mandates

Hearing boards must operate within established legal and procedural frameworks:

1. **Public Institutions & Constitutional Due Process (The Fourteenth Amendment):**
   Under the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, students enrolled in public colleges, universities, and public school districts possess constitutionally protected property and liberty interests in their education, academic standing, and professional reputation. In landmark rulings such as *Goss v. Lopez*, 419 U.S. 565 (1975), and *Board of Curators of the University of Missouri v. Horowitz*, 435 U.S. 78 (1978), the United States Supreme Court established that before a public educational institution can impose disciplinary sanctions—particularly severe penalties such as course failure, suspension, or expulsion—the student is entitled to oral or written notice of the specific charges, an explanation of the evidence possessed by authorities, and an opportunity to present their side of the story in a meaningful, impartial forum. Subjecting a student to academic sanctions based solely on an unexplainable, proprietary algorithmic calculation deprives them of meaningful notice and the ability to confront the evidence against them.

2. **Private Institutions & Contractual Fair Hearing Rights:**
   While private universities and independent preparatory schools are not direct state actors under the Fourteenth Amendment, they are strictly bound by the doctrine of contractual compliance. An institution's student handbook, honor code constitution, and disciplinary bylaws constitute an enforceable express or implied contract between the school and the matriculated student. When an honor code promises students "a fair hearing," "adjudication based on substantive evidence," or "the right to examine all materials entered into the record," relying on opaque black-box detector percentages constitutes a material breach of contract, exposing the institution to civil litigation, injunctions, and costly arbitrations.

3. **FERPA Student Inspection Mandates (34 CFR Part 99 § 99.10):**
   Under the **Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA)**, students (and parents of secondary students under 18) have an absolute federal statutory right to inspect and review all education records directly related to them. This mandate explicitly encompasses disciplinary files, academic integrity referral logs, algorithmic outputs, and all supporting investigative artifacts. If an institution relies on a commercial AI detector that operates as a closed neural network—withholding token logits, attention maps, and training heuristics as proprietary trade secrets—the institution cannot provide the student with the underlying factual basis for their sanction, creating a direct FERPA compliance vulnerability.

### Legal Evidentiary Standards in Academic Hearings

To render defensible decisions, honor boards must ground their deliberations in established standards of proof:

* **Preponderance of the Evidence (Standard Academic Disciplinary Standard):** The standard applied in the vast majority of honor board hearings. The referring party must prove that it is *more likely than not* (greater than a 50% probability) that the student engaged in the alleged academic misconduct. Critically, "preponderance of the evidence" requires **qualitative and factual proof**, not merely a software tool displaying a mathematical probability of 51% or higher.
* **Clear and Convincing Evidence (High-Stakes Suspension/Expulsion Standard):** Required by many university honor codes and secondary school districts when considering severe sanctions such as suspension, dismissal, transcript notation, or degree revocation. This standard demands that the evidence presented be highly and substantially more probable to be true than not, leaving no substantial doubt in the minds of the board members.

Commercial AI detection engines do not detect generative AI directly. Instead, they calculate two linguistic heuristics across static, submitted text:
1. **Perplexity:** A mathematical measure of how "surprised" a language model is by a sequence of words. Lower perplexity indicates that words match the predictable statistical distributions of large language models.
2. **Burstiness:** A measure of sentence length, grammatical structure, and syntactic variation across a document. Uniform, evenly balanced sentences produce low burstiness scores, which detectors treat as a marker of synthetic text.

### The Four Fatal Evidentiary Flaws of AI Detection Scores

When evaluated under formal hearing rules, probabilistic AI detection scores exhibit four fatal structural flaws:

#### 1. Non-Reproducibility and Vendor Model Drift
Unlike chemical forensic tests or mathematical proofs, commercial AI detectors are dynamic, opaque systems. Vendors constantly update their underlying neural weights, training corpuses, and classification thresholds. A document scanned on Monday may return an "85% AI" score, but when re-scanned three weeks later during an honor board hearing, the same document may return a "22% AI" score due to silent vendor updates. An evidentiary metric that cannot be reliably reproduced under identical conditions fails basic legal standards of scientific reliability.

#### 2. Systematic Disparate Impact on ESL/ELL and International Writers
Landmark research (including empirical studies from Stanford University, Liang et al., 2023) has demonstrated that non-native English speakers are systematically penalized by probabilistic AI detectors. English Language Learners (ELL) naturally utilize a more constrained vocabulary, rely on standard grammatical phrasing, and exhibit lower lexical perplexity—the exact statistical features flagged as "machine-generated." Penalizing an international student based on a detector score without process evidence constitutes discriminatory disparate impact.

#### 3. Neurodivergent Composition Penalties
Students with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), ADHD, or specific learning disabilities frequently employ highly structured, systematic, and non-idiomatic writing patterns. Their authentic prose often displays low burstiness and rigorous uniformity. Relying on whole-paper probability scores punishes neurodivergent students for their authentic neurodivergent cognitive styles.

#### 4. The Absence of Primary Source Corroboration ("The Subpoena Problem")
In traditional plagiarism cases, the evidentiary chain is complete: the prosecution points to Text A (student's essay) and Text B (Wikipedia or a published journal article). In generative AI accusations, however, there is no Text B. The language model generates ephemeral, non-indexed sequences of tokens. Because the detector cannot produce the original prompt, the server-side LLM output, or the source transcript, the accusation rests entirely on an algorithmic opinion rather than verifiable primary evidence.

#### Key Telemetry Artifacts Evaluated by Honor Boards:
1. **Scrubbable Keystroke-by-Keystroke Video Replay:** Board members can scrub through the entire writing timeline at 1x, 2x, 4x, or 8x speed, watching the student formulate ideas, draft arguments, delete paragraphs, and refine syntax in real time.

## 8. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

### 1. Why is a high AI detection percentage (e.g., 90% AI) legally insufficient to uphold a plagiarism sanction?
A commercial AI detection percentage is a probabilistic estimate calculated by a neural network based on statistical metrics such as perplexity and burstiness. It is not an empirical measurement of fraud, nor does it produce a primary source artifact. Because these models are non-reproducible, subject to vendor model drift, and carry documented false-positive biases against non-native English speakers and neurodivergent writers, relying on a percentage score alone violates procedural due process and fails the legal standard of a *preponderance of the evidence*.

### 2. How does Checkmark's Essay Playback™ prove a student didn't just retype an AI-generated essay from their phone?
When a student manually retypes text from a second screen, phone, or printed sheet, their keystroke dynamics exhibit distinct mechanical patterns: uniform typing velocity, exceptionally low Inter-Key Interval (IKI) variance, and an absence of natural cognitive formulation pauses at complex conceptual boundaries. Furthermore, manual transcription displays a near-zero revision friction rate (lack of backspacing, sentence re-structuring, or vocabulary substitutions). Checkmark's telemetry dashboard flags these mechanical transcription anomalies instantly.

### 3. What is the difference between an uncredited source and deliberate plagiarism during an appeal?
Checkmark’s dual-pane viewer differentiates between malicious source copying and inadvertent citation errors using its 4-badge taxonomy. An `Uncited Source` flag indicates that source text was incorporated without proper attribution tags, which often points to citation formatting ignorance or poor note-taking. Conversely, `Exact Match` and `Paraphrased Match` flags tied to large external paste buffer records with deceptive synonym swapping demonstrate deliberate intent to misrepresent authorship.

### 4. How does the 72-hour dossier disclosure window satisfy federal FERPA regulations?
Under FERPA (34 CFR Part 99 § 99.10), students have the right to inspect and review all education records used in disciplinary decisions. By providing the appellant with an unredacted, interactive Checkmark Appeals Dossier 72 hours before the hearing, the institution guarantees the student's statutory inspection rights, eliminates "trial by ambush," and allows the student to prepare a targeted, evidence-based defense.

### 5. Can a student falsely claim that a large paste was simply their own pre-drafted notes?
Yes, this is a common defense during appeals. However, Checkmark eliminates ambiguity through its ecosystem capture and paste forensics. Student essays, draft revisions, and keystroke logs are never used to train commercial AI models or shared with third-party vendors.
