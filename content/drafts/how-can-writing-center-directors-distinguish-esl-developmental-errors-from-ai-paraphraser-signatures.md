### 1. Perplexity ($PPL$) and the Bounded Lexicon
Perplexity measures how "surprised" a language model is by a sequence of words. A human writer with native English mastery frequently deploys uncommon collocations, cultural idioms, conversational metaphors, and rare vocabulary words, generating high perplexity. 

In contrast, an English Language Learner naturally operates within a **bounded lexicon**—a curated mental inventory of high-frequency academic vocabulary acquired through textbooks, vocabulary lists, and formal instruction (e.g., *furthermore, in addition, consequently, it is important to consider*). Because their word selections are mathematically predictable and statistically common, an AI detector calculates a very low perplexity score, concluding the text was generated by a probabilistic language model.

### 2. Burstiness ($B$) and Syntactic Regularity
Burstiness quantifies the variation in sentence length, grammatical architecture, and rhythmic cadence across a document. Experienced native writers naturally intersperse short, punchy declarative statements with lengthy, multi-clause compound-complex sentences.

Multilingual writers, striving for grammatical clarity and precision while managing heavy cognitive load, gravitate toward safe, predictable sentence blueprints—typically Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) constructions averaging 15 to 22 words per sentence. This low variation in sentence length produces a low burstiness score, mirroring the smooth, homogenous sentence distribution characteristic of raw LLM outputs.

### 1. Defining Interlanguage: The Systematic Logic of Second-Language Acquisition
Coined by linguist Larry Selinker in 1972, **Interlanguage** represents the separate, dynamic linguistic system created by an individual acquiring a second language ($L_2$). Crucially, interlanguage is not an arbitrary collection of mistakes; it is a rule-governed, systematic grammar influenced by:
* **$L_1$ Language Transfer (Calques):** The direct transposition of the syntactic rules, prepositions, or discourse structures of the native language into English. For example, a native Spanish speaker may write *"The proposal of the committee"* rather than *"The committee's proposal"* due to Spanish noun-phrase architecture (*la propuesta del comité*). A native Mandarin speaker may omit definite articles (*the*) because Mandarin lacks morphological equivalents.
* **Overgeneralization of $L_2$ Rules:** Applying a newly learned English grammatical rule where it does not belong (e.g., regularizing irregular past-tense verbs like *"teached"* instead of *"taught"* or adding plural markers to non-count nouns like *"researches"* or *"informations"*).
* **Formulaic Scaffolding:** Leaning heavily on introductory transition templates (*"First and foremost," "On the other hand," "To summarize the above points"*) to organize thoughts.

In authentic interlanguage writing, **the conceptual core remains intact**. Even when surface-level morphosyntactic errors occur, the student’s underlying thought progression is discernible, human, and unified.

### 2. Deconstructing AI Paraphrasers: The Anatomy of "Rogeting" & Semantic Drift
Commercial AI paraphrasers—such as QuillBot, SpinBot, WordTune, and so-called "AI text humanizers"—do not compose through communicative intent. Instead, they ingest existing text strings and execute algorithmic perturbation:
* **Thesaurus Substitution ("Rogeting"):** Replacing content words with statistical synonyms extracted from vector embeddings. Because the algorithm lacks deep semantic comprehension of the specific academic discipline, it selects words that are technically synonymous in a general dictionary but nonsensical in scholarly discourse.
  * *Original:* "The economic policies caused significant inflation."
  * *Paraphrased:* "The fiscal guidelines initiated momentous swelling."
* **Collocation Fracture:** In human language, words co-occur in predictable pairings known as collocations (e.g., *"conduct research," "raise an objection," "deeply concerned"*). AI paraphrasers frequently break these natural pairings, producing syntactically plausible but rhetorically absurd phrases (e.g., *"execute investigations," "elevate a protestation," "profoundly troubled"*).
* **Semantic Drift and Contradiction:** When an entire paragraph is run through multiple iterations of paraphrase spinning, the core thesis drifts. Negations are accidentally dropped, causal relationships are inverted, and historical or empirical claims become distorted.
* **Register Collision (Mosaic Jargon):** AI paraphrasers often graft archaic, hyper-formal vocabulary (*heretofore, amongst, ostensibly, bespoke*) onto informal or basic sentence frames, resulting in an unnatural stylistic dissonance that no human writer—native or non-native—would produce spontaneously.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### 1. Why do commercial AI detectors flag ESL and international student writing at such disproportionate rates?
Commercial AI detectors evaluate text based on **Perplexity** (word-choice unpredictability) and **Burstiness** (sentence-length variation). Multilingual writers naturally utilize a more constrained, high-frequency academic vocabulary and construct sentences with consistent, predictable lengths. Because these features match the statistical probability distributions of large language models, detectors misclassify authentic non-native English as machine-generated text.

### 2. What is the fundamental linguistic difference between an interlanguage developmental error and an AI paraphraser signature?
An **interlanguage error** stems from systematic, rule-governed second-language acquisition, often reflecting direct translation rules ($L_1$ calques) or overgeneralized grammar. Despite minor surface errors, the underlying argument is logically sound. An **AI paraphraser signature** stems from context-blind synonym replacement ("Rogeting"), which preserves surface grammatical complexity while introducing bizarre word pairings (collocation fractures), register collisions, and nonsensical semantic drift.

### 3. Can a student fake authentic typing dynamics by retyping text from an AI paraphraser on their phone?
No. Writing telemetry in Checkmark's **Essay Playback™** captures the cognitive reality of composing. When a student copies or transcribes text from a secondary screen, they type at a flat, robotic velocity (60–90 WPM) with virtually no backspacing friction and an absolute absence of natural cognitive pause clusters ($P$-bursts). In contrast, authentic writers exhibit frequent pauses (5 to 45 seconds) before complex vocabulary, recursive word corrections, and uneven typing bursts.

### 4. Is using Google Translate or DeepL considered academic dishonesty in college writing?
Policies vary by institution and department, but most writing programs distinguish between **translation scaffolding** (using translation tools to find the English equivalent of an independently conceived thought) and **generative authorship substitution** (using an LLM to generate arguments). Machine translation preserves the student's original $L_1$ ideas and syntactic logic, whereas generative AI replaces student cognition entirely.

### 5. How should a writing center tutor respond if an instructor sends a student with an "85% AI" Turnitin score?
Tutors should first reassure the student that automated scores are statistical estimates prone to high false-positive rates on multilingual prose. The tutor should then review the student's draft using **Essay Playback™** to verify authentic drafting effort, conduct a metacognitive conversation about the essay's core ideas, and generate a Checkmark Telemetry Summary to help the instructor understand the authentic human labor behind the submission.

### 6. What are "calques" and how do they prove human authorship in multilingual essays?
A **calque** (or loan translation) occurs when a writer translates a phrase directly from their native language into English word-for-word, preserving the source language’s unique grammatical structure or metaphorical logic. Because Large Language Models are trained on massive corpora of standardized English and generate native-like fluency, the presence of genuine $L_1$ calques strongly indicates authentic, human non-native drafting.

### 7. What is "Rogeting" and why is it common in AI paraphrasing tools?
"Rogeting" refers to the automated, mechanical substitution of words with synonyms from a thesaurus without regard for contextual nuance. Commercial paraphrasers like QuillBot and SpinBot use algorithmic synonym graphs that frequently swap standard academic terms for unnatural or absurd alternatives (e.g., replacing *"greenhouse gases"* with *"verdant structure vapors"*), creating telltale linguistic signatures.
