## 1. The Disciplinary Dilemma: Generative AI in the Music Theory Classroom

For centuries, music theory pedagogy has relied on a foundational pedagogical contract: the student analyzes a musical score, grapples with pitch relationships and formal syntax at the keyboard or through internal audiation, and writes a detailed textual defense justifying their analytical decisions. Whether constructing a four-voice Bach chorale realization in undergraduate *Theory II*, drafting strict species counterpoint in a conservatory Renaissance seminar, or annotating a Brahms intermezzo for an AP Music Theory portfolio, written prose has served as the window into a student's cognitive and aural comprehension.

The advent of frontier multimodal large language models (such as GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Gemini 1.5 Pro) has fundamentally fractured this evaluative window. 

Today, a student can upload a high-resolution smartphone photo of a hand-annotated score, an exported PDF from Sibelius, Dorico, or MuseScore, or a raw MusicXML text file into an LLM and generate a multi-page, scholarly analytical paper in under fifteen seconds. The resulting prose is syntactically flawless, effortlessly deploying Italian, German, and French musical terminology, quoting Schenkerian linear concepts, citing formal-prall conventions, and weaving historical context into measure-by-measure commentary.

### 2.1 Roman Numeral Analysis & Pivot Chord Confabulations

When tasked with providing a Roman numeral analysis (RNA) of a modulating passage—such as a transition in a Classical sonata-allegro exposition from tonic ($I$) to dominant ($V$), or a Romantic modulation to a chromatic mediant ($lat VI$ or $\sharp III$)—LLMs frequently generate logically structured but musically fictional pivot chord explanations.

#### The Mechanics of the Hallucination:
- **Phantom Diatonic Overlaps:** The model asserts that a specific measure functions as a dual-identity pivot chord (e.g., $	ext{vi}$ in E-flat Major = $	ext{iv}$ in G minor) when the pitch collection in that measure contains accidentals that make the chord diatonic in neither key.
- **Inverted Root Logic:** The model confuses figured bass inversions with root-position chords, describing a cadential $^{6}_{4}$ chord as an independent tonic triad in second inversion ($I^{6}_{4}$) exercising tonic stability, rather than recognizing its dominant syntactic function ($V^{6-5}_{4-3}$).
- **Secondary Dominant Misidentifications:** LLMs routinely label any chord containing an accidental as a secondary dominant ($V^7/V$ or $V^7/	ext{iv}$), even when the chord represents modal mixture (borrowed chord), an applied diminished seventh ($	ext{vii}^{\circ 7}$), or a purely linear chromatic passing sonority.

### 2.2 Species Counterpoint Rule Violations Masked by Eloquent Rhetoric

In strict Fuxian species counterpoint (First through Fifth Species), students are evaluated on rigorous, centuries-old contrapuntal rules: prohibition of parallel fifths and octaves, avoidance of direct (hidden) fifths/octaves, restriction of harmonic dissonances to specific metric placements (passing tones on weak beats in Second Species, accented suspensions in Fourth Species), and maintenance of a single, well-defined melodic climax in the *cantus firmus*.

Generative AI models excel at reciting these rules in prose while failing completely to enforce them when generating or analyzing two-, three-, or four-part counterpoint.

### 2.3 Chromatic Harmony: Augmented Sixth and Neapolitan Confabulations

Advanced tonal harmony portfolios require students to identify and rationalize chromatic pre-dominant sonorities: Neapolitan sixth chords ($	ext{N}^6$ or $lat 	ext{II}^6$) and the three national variants of augmented sixth chords:
- **Italian Sixth ($	ext{It}^{+6}$):** $lat \hat{6} - \hat{1} - \sharp \hat{4}$ (three pitch classes; tonic doubled).
- **French Sixth ($	ext{Fr}^{+6}$):** $lat \hat{6} - \hat{1} - \hat{2} - \sharp \hat{4}$ (contains the whole-tone scale degree $\hat{2}$).
- **German Sixth ($	ext{Ger}^{+6}$):** $lat \hat{6} - \hat{1} - lat \hat{3} - \sharp \hat{4}$ (enharmonically equivalent to a dominant seventh chord).

LLMs struggle notoriously with the enharmonic spelling and voice-leading mechanics of these chords. Common AI confabulations include:
1. **Misidentifying the Augmented Sixth Interval:** The model confuses the characteristic augmented sixth interval ($lat \hat{6}$ in the bass resolving outward to $\hat{5}$, and $\sharp \hat{4}$ in an upper voice resolving outward to $\hat{5}$) with a minor seventh, mislabeling a German augmented sixth chord as a secondary dominant seventh ($V^7/lat 	ext{VI}$ or $V^7/	ext{IV}$).
2. **Fabricating the "Mozart Fifth" Resolution:** The model will claim in the written text that parallel fifths occurring between the bass ($lat \hat{6}$) and tenor ($lat \hat{3}$) in a German sixth resolving directly to $V$ were "carefully avoided through the insertion of a cadential $^{6}_{4}$ or Italian variant," even when the student's score contains raw, unmitigated parallel fifths.
3. **Neapolitan Inversion Errors:** The model will describe a Neapolitan chord as resolving directly to tonic ($I$) in root position, failing to recognize that authentic Neapolitan syntax operates almost exclusively as a pre-dominant in first inversion ($	ext{N}^6$) resolving to $V$ or $V^7$, often via an intervening cadential $^{6}_{4}$ or $	ext{vii}^{\circ 7}/V$.

### 2.4 Fabricated Voice-Leading Rationales & Historical Confabulations

When students make fundamental part-writing errors in SATB voice leading—such as voice crossing (tenor singing higher than alto), voice overlapping (alto moving below the previous pitch of the tenor), or doubling the leading tone ($\hat{7}$)—generative AI models frequently generate apologetic, pseudo-scholarly rationalizations:

> *"The temporary doubling of the leading tone in measure 11 is utilized deliberately as an expressive rhetorical device (pathopeia) to heighten the affective tension of the text, evoking 17th-century stylus fantasticus practices rather than strict Common Practice part-writing conventions."*

In the context of an undergraduate *Theory I* or *II* four-part voice-leading assignment where the rubric strictly mandates common-practice doubling rules (double the root in root position; never double the leading tone), this high-sounding prose masks a simple student blunder. The AI manufactures historical justifications that no freshman music major would independently conceptualize, providing a clear marker of unauthorized generative intervention.

### 2.5 Post-Tonal & Pitch-Class Set Theoretic Miscalculations

In 20th-century and contemporary music analysis portfolios (involving Allen Forte’s pitch-class set theory, prime forms, and interval class vectors), LLMs exhibit rampant mathematical hallucinations:
- **Incorrect Prime Form Calculation:** An LLM will ingest a pitch set such as $[C, C\sharp, E, F\sharp]$ (pitch classes $[0, 1, 4, 6]$) and identify its prime form as `[0, 1, 4, 8]` (Forte number `4-19`) instead of the correct prime form `[0, 1, 3, 7]` (Forte number `4-Z15`).
- **Interval Class Vector (ICV) Confabulation:** The model will generate an interval vector string (e.g., `<1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1>`) that does not mathematically sum to the correct number of dyadic intervals ($n(n-1)/2$).
- **Twelve-Tone Matrix Inversions:** When analyzing serialist works (such as Schoenberg, Berg, or Webern), LLMs routinely miscalculate the retrograde-inversion ($RI$) or inverted ($I$) row forms, transposing intervals incorrectly while maintaining an authoritative tone in the explanatory text.

### 3.1 The Standardized Terminology False Positive Trap

Music analysis is written in a highly stylized, disciplinary dialect. Phrases such as:
- *"The soprano line establishes a linear descent from scale degree 5 to scale degree 1..."*
- *"The cadential six-four chord on the strong beat functions as an embellishment of the dominant..."*
- *"The modulation to the dominant is achieved via a common-chord pivot in measure 18..."*
- *"Contrary motion between the outer voices prevents parallel fifths at the cadence..."*

are repeated identically across thousands of music theory textbooks (Kostka-Payne, Clendinning-Marvin, Aldwell-Schachter, Laitz). 

Legacy text-matching engines (such as standard Turnitin similarity indices) ingest these necessary theoretical formulas and highlight them in red as "unoriginal plagiarized text" copied from Wikipedia, IMSLP forum threads, or academic papers. Concurrently, generic AI detectors analyze the low perplexity (predictability) of these standard theoretical phrases and assign high AI probability scores to authentic student writing, falsely accusing diligent students.

### 3.2 The Short-Annotation Problem (<150 Words)

In real-world music theory portfolios, students rarely write monolithic 3,000-word uninterrupted essays. Instead, portfolio submissions are structured modularly:
- A 2-page engraved PDF score in Dorico or Sibelius.
- 10 to 20 discrete measure-by-measure callout boxes or analytical annotations, each comprising 30 to 120 words explaining a specific harmonic shift, formal juncture, or voice-leading decision.

Generic AI detectors are mathematically uncalibrated for text samples under 150–250 words. When fed a 60-word explanation of an enharmonic modulation, generic detectors output extreme, erratic probabilities (e.g., fluctuating between 98% AI and 0% AI upon changing a single word). Relying on these tools to discipline students violates institutional due process and damages faculty-student trust.

### 3.3 The Notational Blind Spot

A generic text-analysis tool evaluates prose in complete isolation from the musical score it purports to explain. If a student submits an AI-generated essay that perfectly describes a Bach chorale analysis in G Major, but the student’s actual submitted score is in F Major with different measure numbers and different voice leading, a generic detector gives no indication of this fatal discrepancy. 

Only a multi-dimensional evidentiary workflow—combining score-to-prose concordance, keystroke writing process history, and authentic portfolio telemetry—can uncover the truth.

### Stage 1: The Score-to-Prose Concordance Audit

The primary indicator of AI-generated music analysis is a direct factual contradiction between what is written in the prose commentary and what appears on the musical staff.

#### Faculty Checklist for Concordance:
1. **Measure Number Alignment:** Do the harmonic events described in the prose match the exact measures in the score? (LLMs frequently hallucinate measure offsets, describing a cadence in measure 16 that actually occurs in measure 14).
2. **Pitch-Class Accuracy:** Does the prose describe accidentals ($F\sharp$, $Blat$) or specific chord members that do not exist in the notated chord?
3. **Key and Transposition Integrity:** If the score is an excerpt from a Beethoven sonata transposed to $F$ Major for pedagogical purposes, does the text discuss the piece in its original key of $C$ minor? (A classic sign of an AI prompt where the model pulled analysis of the famous original work rather than reading the transposed student score).
4. **Figured Bass and Inversion Check:** Does the text claim a chord is in root position when the score clearly places the third or fifth in the bass?

## 8. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### 1. How can music faculty tell if a student used AI to generate their Roman numeral analysis?
Look for **Score-to-Prose Incongruity**. Multimodal LLMs routinely hallucinate chord roots, mislabel figured bass inversions, invent phantom pivot chords in distant modulations, and confuse chromatic mediants with diatonic submediants. When the written commentary describes accidentals, chord tones, or voice resolutions that do not exist in the submitted score notation, the analysis was likely generated by an AI model.

### 2. Why do generic AI detectors flag authentic music theory essays as AI-generated?
Music theory relies heavily on formulaic, highly standardized terminology (*"cadential six-four resolving to dominant seventh," "contrary motion to an imperfect consonance"*). Generic AI detectors evaluate text based on perplexity (word unpredictability). Because standard musical analysis uses highly predictable academic phrasing, generic detectors mistake authentic student writing for machine generation, producing rampant false positives.

### 3. How does Checkmark’s Essay Playback™ verify authentic music theory drafting?
Essay Playback™ reconstructs the entire writing session keystroke by keystroke. In an authentic music analysis session, students exhibit characteristic **piano-testing pauses** (30–120 seconds of typing inactivity while testing chords on a keyboard or checking a score), non-linear revisions (deleting and correcting Roman numerals), and measure-by-measure pacing. Conversely, AI use appears as instantaneous bulk pastes or rapid, uninterrupted transcription without pauses or edits.

### 4. What if a student claims they drafted their harmonic analysis offline or in a physical notebook?
If a student drafted their analysis on paper and typed it into the document in one sitting, their keystroke dynamics will show a continuous typing stream. However, unlike AI generation, authentic typing from handwritten notes still contains natural human typos, spontaneous word substitutions, and formatting adjustments. Furthermore, faculty can conduct a brief, restorative 5-minute conference at the piano, where an authentic student can easily explain and play their harmonic reasoning.

### 5. Can AI accurately analyze complex polyphonic music, such as a 5-voice fugue or late-Romantic chromaticism?
No. Current frontier LLMs struggle significantly with polyphonic voice-tracking, spatial score navigation, and complex chromatic syntax (such as Wagnerian Tristan chords, omnibus progressions, or post-tonal pitch-class sets). While the AI will produce fluent, scholarly prose, the detailed voice-leading claims will almost always contain acoustic and notational hallucinations.
