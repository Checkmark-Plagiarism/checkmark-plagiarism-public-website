### Understanding Neurodivergent Cognitive Modalities

The neurodiversity paradigm recognizes that neurodevelopmental variations—including Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), Autism Spectrum Condition (ASC), executive dysfunction, dyslexia, and dysgraphia—are natural, valid expressions of human cognitive diversity. In the domain of written expression, these conditions manifest in distinct, highly effective, yet non-traditional drafting strategies:

#### 1. ADHD and Hyperfocus/Bimodal Drafting
Writers with ADHD frequently experience fluctuations in dopamine regulation and executive functioning that manifest as either task-initiation paralysis or intense states of **hyperfocus**. During a hyperfocus episode, an ADHD writer may draft 600 words of dense, highly creative analysis in a 20-minute surge of rapid keystrokes, completely bypassing conventional outlining. Conversely, organizing transitions or structuring introductions may require hours of silent cognitive incubation, during which no visible typing occurs on screen.

#### 2. Autism and Modular "Bricolage" Construction
Autistic writers often conceptualize arguments modularly or "bottom-up"—assembling detailed factual nodes, specialized domain evidence, and primary source analyses before determining the overarching thematic umbrella. They frequently write the most complex evidentiary sections first, only later drafting introductory scaffolding to connect the components.

#### 3. Executive Dysfunction and Multi-Window Scratchpads
Students with executive functioning challenges often struggle with working memory overload when drafting directly inside a formal, formatted document. To manage cognitive load, they distribute their drafting process across multiple lightweight scratchpads: Apple Notes, Google Keep, Notion workspaces, or personal Discord servers. They develop modular paragraph blocks in isolation and copy-paste them into the primary LMS or Google Docs submission file when ready to assemble the final paper.

#### 4. Dysgraphia, Dyslexia, and Speech-to-Text Dictation
Students with dysgraphia (fine-motor coordination and written expression impairments) or dyslexia frequently utilize assistive technologies, such as speech-to-text (STT) voice dictation software (e.g., Apple Dictation, Google Voice Typing, or Dragon NaturallySpeaking). This results in large blocks of transcribed prose entering the document rapidly, followed by extensive cursor jumping, phonological spell-checking, and sentence-level syntactical reordering via keyboard.

### Failure 1: The "Abrupt Block" Illusion in Coarse Revision Histories

Standard document management systems (such as default Google Docs version history or Canvas LMS document snapshots) do not record continuous, high-resolution writing telemetry. Instead, they capture periodic "deltas" or batched revisions every few minutes.

When a student who struggles with executive dysfunction drafts a 300-word paragraph in a separate note-taking application and pastes it into their primary essay document, the revision history records an instantaneous 300-word jump. 

* **The Incompetent Delta Scanner Conclusion:** *"A 300-word block was pasted with zero preceding keystrokes; therefore, the student pasted generative AI output or plagiarized text."*

Because coarse tools fail to preserve the clipboard payload or visualize subsequent in-document micro-revisions, the educator is left with an alarming, misleading artifact that looks identical to an illicit ChatGPT copy-paste.

Static AI classifiers and primitive keystroke loggers assume that human typing adheres to a smooth, Gaussian bell-curve distribution of velocity. They expect a student to type at a steady, continuous pace of 35 to 50 words per minute with evenly distributed pauses.

In writing telemetry research, the **Inter-Key Interval ($IKI$)** measures the millisecond latency between consecutive keystrokes:

$$IKI_k = t_{k} - t_{k-1}$$

Where $t_k$ is the UNIX timestamp of keypress event $k$.

* **Hyperfocus Burst ($IKI < 140\text{ ms}$):** When an ADHD or autistic writer reaches cognitive clarity, their drafting velocity spikes dramatically. Keystrokes are registered in rapid-fire bursts with near-zero latency.
* **Deep Cognitive Processing Pause ($IKI > 10{,}000\text{ ms}$):** Between these bursts, the writer may sit completely idle for 5 to 15 minutes—staring at the ceiling, pacing the room, rereading notes, or mentally constructing the next argument.

Opaque, statistical AI detectors misinterpret this high-velocity drafting as synthetic token streaming, while simplistic delta tools assume the text was injected via macro or clipboard automation. Without millisecond-level telemetry, authentic cognitive flow is condemned as algorithmic generation.

### 1. Why do generic AI detectors fail so disproportionately on neurodivergent writing styles?
Generic AI detectors analyze static text using statistical metrics called **Perplexity** (word predictability) and **Burstiness** (sentence length diversity). Neurodivergent writers frequently produce prose with unique statistical signatures:
* Autistic writers and individuals with ADHD who hyperfocus on specialized vocabulary may write with high technical precision and formal sentence structures, which detectors misclassify as "low perplexity" (AI-like).
* Students who struggle with executive dysfunction may use clear, predictable transitional formulas to anchor their thoughts, resulting in "low burstiness."
Because generic detectors look only at the static end-product rather than the dynamic writing journey, they confuse focused, disciplined human drafting with machine-generated output.

### 2. How does Essay Playback™ distinguish between a student pasting their own notes vs. pasting ChatGPT text?
Checkmark's **External Paste Buffer Tracking** captures the exact raw clipboard payload at the millisecond of insertion. If a student pastes their own rough notes, the inspector reveals disjointed bullet points, personal shorthand, grammatical fragments, and informal brainstorm queries. Furthermore, the **Essay Playback™** timeline reveals what happens next: a student working from their own notes spends substantial time (e.g., 20–60 minutes) actively typing, expanding fragments, and micro-editing sentences. In contrast, an unauthorized AI copy-paste consists of fully polished, finalized paragraphs requiring zero subsequent compositional labor.
