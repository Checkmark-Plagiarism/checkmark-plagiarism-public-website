### 2.3 Morphosyntactic Signatures of Machine Translation Across Target Languages

Machine-translated and LLM-generated L2 texts exhibit specific linguistic anomalies that clash violently with natural learner developmental trajectories. Understanding these markers helps instructors spot inconsistencies during portfolio evaluations.

#### Spanish (Español)
- **Premature Subjunctive Mastery:** Intermediate-Low learners spontaneously producing complex hypothetical clauses with pluperfect subjunctive and conditional perfect (*"Si me hubiera enterado de la verdad, no habría actuado de esa manera"*), when course benchmarks only cover the present indicative and preterite/imperfect distinction.
- **Unnatural Clitic and Passive Constructions:** Overuse of passive voice (*"El libro fue escrito por..."*) instead of authentic reflexive passive (*"Se escribió el libro..."*), or hyper-correct placement of double object pronouns (*"Se lo entregué"* without hesitation).
- **L1 Calques:** Translating *"to make a decision"* as *"hacer una decisión"* (literal) vs. machine translation rendering the idiomatic *"tomar una decisión"* alongside vocabulary 3 levels above the student's speaking ability.

#### French (Français)
- **Complex Hypothetical & Subjunctive Sequencing:** Flawless use of the *subjonctif passé* or *conditionnel passé deuxième forme* in Level II/III submissions.
- **Partitive vs. Definite Article Precision:** Zero errors in partitive transformations under negation (*"Je n'ai pas de..."* vs. *"Je n'ai pas des..."*), an error that persists across authentic Intermediate and Advanced-Low writers.
- **False Friend Handling:** Sophisticated contextual disambiguation of words like *actuellement* (currently vs. actually) or *assister à* that learners routinely confuse.

#### German (Deutsch)
- **Subordinate Clause Verb-Final Perfection (Kangsatz / Nebensatz):** Flawless recursive subordinate clauses with multi-part verb clusters at the absolute end (*"...weil er gestern trotz des schweren Sturms pünktlich an der Universität angekommen sein soll"*).
- **Adjective Declension & Case Congruence:** Complete absence of weak/strong/mixed adjective inflection errors across dative and genitive cases—an area where human learners exhibit frequent slips of the pen.
- **Modal Particle Injections:** Inappropriate placement of subtle native conversational particles (*doch, ja, denn, halt, mal*) rarely mastered prior to C1/ACTFL Superior levels.

#### Mandarin Chinese (中文 - 汉语)
- **Advanced Measure Words (量词):** Effortless integration of highly specific, rare measure words (*一尾鱼*, *一封信*, *一栋建筑*) alongside novice-level discourse structures.
- **Four-Character Idiomatic Expressions (成语 - Chéngyǔ):** Insertion of classical idioms (*半途而废*, *津津有味*, *循序渐进*) in simple narrative essays about daily school life.
- **Complex Directional & Resultative Complements:** Intricate constructions (*跑了进去*, *听得懂*, *拿不出来*) without a single character stroke deletion or IME phonetic correction during typing.

#### Italian (Italiano) & Japanese (日本語)
- **Italian:** Flawless concordance of past subjunctive (*congiuntivo imperfetto/trapassato*) after verbs of opinion (*"Non pensavo che tu fossi già arrivato"*).
- **Japanese:** Flawless switching between plain form (*da/dearu*), polite form (*desu/masu*), and complex honorifics (*keigo* - *sonkeigo* / *kenjougo*), alongside immaculate particle selection (*wa* vs. *ga*, *ni* vs. *de*).

1. **The Perplexity Penalty on Novice Writers:** Statistical AI detectors measure **perplexity** (word predictability). Because beginning and intermediate language learners write with simple, highly predictable vocabulary, their essays naturally score in the lowest perplexity percentiles. Statistical tools interpret this formulaic simplicity as an LLM signature, generating devastating false accusations against non-native writers.
2. **The Burstiness Paradox:** Human native writers vary their sentence lengths dynamically (high **burstiness**), whereas language learners write in short, uniform declarative sentences. Because generic AI detectors associate low burstiness with automated text generation, authentic student essays are systematically flagged.
3. **The DeepL Cloaking Effect:** DeepL transforms basic English thoughts into rich, varied, idiomatic L2 sentences. The resulting text exhibits high perplexity and burstiness. Consequently, statistical AI detectors pass machine-translated work with clean scores, allowing genuine academic dishonesty to slip through undetected.

### 5.1 Authentic L2 Drafting Signatures

1. **The "Burst-and-Pause" Cadence (10–25 WPM Average):** In a developing language, writers cannot compose continuously. They type in short syntactic bursts (2–5 words), followed by cognitive micro-pauses (1.5–5.0 seconds) to retrieve vocabulary, select gender agreements, or verify verb endings.
2. **Morphological and Orthographic Backspacing:** Genuine L2 writers make frequent inflectional corrections. In Spanish, an authentic student might type *"hablaba"*, immediately backspace three characters, and type *"é"* (*hablé*) as they consciously decide between the preterite and imperfect. In German, a student might type a noun, pause, backspace, and capitalize the initial letter to satisfy German orthographic rules.
3. **Lexical Lookup Pauses:** When an authentic learner accesses a digital dictionary (e.g., WordReference), the playback timeline records a brief 5–15 second pause, followed by the deliberate, character-by-character typing of a single isolated word.
4. **IME & Accent Tool Interactions:** In French, Spanish, German, Italian, Mandarin, or Japanese, authentic drafting shows consistent interaction with accent menus, keyboard hotkeys, or IME conversion bars (such as selecting appropriate Chinese Hanzi from Pinyin candidate lists).

### 5.2 Translation Abuse Signatures

1. **The Monolithic Paste Event:** The student drafts their essay in English in another window, translates it via DeepL, copies the target-language output, and pastes 100–300 words into the LMS editor in a single 0.0-second event. Checkmark’s external paste inspector captures the exact timestamp, character count, and full preserved text of the paste.
2. **The "Copy-Paste-Tweak" Mask:** Some students attempt to circumvent paste detection by pasting a machine-translated paragraph and then manually changing 3–4 words (e.g., swapping a synonym or fixing an obvious noun). Checkmark’s timeline captures both the initial massive paste and the subsequent minor superficial edits, preserving the original pasted string in the forensic inspector.
3. **The Second-Screen Transcription Fingerprint:** To avoid paste detection altogether, a student might place a phone or second monitor next to their keyboard displaying Google Translate, manually retyping the translated foreign text into the assignment editor. This creates a distinct **transcription signature**:
   - High, unbroken typing velocity (45–70 WPM) far exceeding the student’s oral fluency.
   - Isochronous (perfectly uniform) inter-keystroke intervals without natural cognitive pauses.
   - Zero inflectional backspacing—the student copies the complex foreign words letter-by-letter without ever pausing to deliberate on verb conjugation or case agreement.

## 9. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

### 1. How does Checkmark Essay Playback handle non-Latin keyboards, such as Mandarin Pinyin IMEs, Japanese Kana, or Cyrillic?
Checkmark's telemetry engine natively captures input method editor (IME) composition events across all major operating systems (macOS, Windows, iOS, ChromeOS). When a student types in Mandarin Chinese using Pinyin, the playback timeline records the phonetic keystrokes, candidate selection interactions, and character conversions. This allows educators to verify that the student actively composed the characters rather than pasting pre-converted Hanzi blocks.

### 2. Can Essay Playback differentiate between looking up a word in WordReference versus translating an entire sentence in DeepL?
Yes, distinctly. When a student uses an approved online dictionary like WordReference, Linguee, or Leo, the telemetry timeline displays a brief 5–15 second pause, followed by the deliberate, character-by-character manual typing of an isolated word (1–2 words). In contrast, machine translation usage produces either a 0.0-second clipboard injection of an entire multi-sentence clause (30–150+ words) or a mechanical transcription pattern where complex multi-word idioms are typed without a single pause or inflectional correction.

### 3. What if a student claims they drafted their foreign language essay offline in Apple Notes or Microsoft Word and pasted it into the LMS?
Checkmark offers native extensions and integrations for Google Docs, Canvas LMS, Buzz LMS, and Microsoft Word. However, if a student pastes an entire essay into the LMS at once from an untracked third-party app, Checkmark’s paste inspector flags the monolithic injection and preserves the full pasted text. Under a standardized department policy, students are instructed to draft within tracked environments. If an offline paste occurs, the teacher can review the student's historical baseline profile and conduct a brief 3-minute oral check-in to confirm the student can explain the grammar and vocabulary used in the pasted text.

### 4. How do writing process replays protect English Language Learners (ELL / ESL) and non-native writers from false AI accusations?
Non-native writers frequently use formulaic sentence structures, repetitive transitions, and limited lexical palettes, which generic probabilistic AI detectors routinely misidentify as AI-generated text. Checkmark protects non-native students by providing undeniable, empirical proof of human authorship: the complete timeline replay showing keystrokes, deletions, dictionary pauses, and sentence restructuring. This physical evidence completely overrides statistical guesswork and protects students from unfair disciplinary action.

### 5. How does Checkmark distinguish between speech-to-text voice dictation in the target language and pasted machine translation?
Voice dictation produces text insertion in discrete multi-word acoustic bursts (typically 3–8 words every few seconds) accompanied by distinct speech-engine correction artifacts (e.g., auto-punctuating or replacing homophones). Monolithic machine translation pastes appear as massive single-timestamp blocks (50–300+ words in 0.0 seconds). If a student with an IEP or 504 accommodation uses approved target-language speech-to-text tools, the cadence exhibits natural oral phrasing pauses rather than instantaneous clipboard dumps.

### 6. Can students bypass draft replay by using "AI Humanizers" or text spinners on their machine-translated foreign language text?
No. While tools like QuillBot or "Undetectable AI" modify vocabulary and syntax to evade static probabilistic detectors, they cannot fake authentic human writing telemetry. A student who runs a translated French or Spanish essay through a humanizer must still either paste the resulting text into the LMS (generating an immediate paste flag in Checkmark) or retype it manually (generating an identifiable mechanical transcription signature with zero natural linguistic reflection pauses).

### 7. Does Checkmark store student language portfolios to train commercial AI models?
No. Checkmark maintains a strict zero-model-training policy. Student submissions, keystroke telemetry, drafts, and portfolio artifacts are never used to train public or proprietary AI models. Checkmark is fully FERPA and COPPA compliant, utilizing enterprise-grade encryption in transit and at rest to ensure that student writing remains entirely confidential and protected within the school or university ecosystem.
